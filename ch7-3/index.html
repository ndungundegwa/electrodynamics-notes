    7.3 Maxwell's Equations#

    The problem before Maxwell#

    After Faraday, the four laws were

    \[\div{\vec{E}} = \frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}, \quad \div{\vec{B}} = 0, \quad \curl{\vec{E}} = -\pdv{\vec{B}}{t}, \quad \curl{\vec{B}} = \mu_0\vec{J}\]

    and the last one contradicts itself. Take the divergence of both sides. The left side is always zero (the divergence of a curl is zero). The right side is \( \mu_0\div{\vec{J}} \), which by the continuity equation is \( -\mu_0\pdv{\rho}{t} \). That is only zero if charge isn't piling up anywhere, i.e. for steady currents.

    You can see the problem with a charging capacitor. Ampère's law says: the line integral of \( \vec{B} \) round a loop is \( \mu_0 \) times the current through any surface with that loop as its edge. Put a loop round the wire leading to the capacitor.

    • Choose a flat surface that the wire passes through: the answer is \( \mu_0 I \).
    • Choose a surface that bulges out like a bag and passes between the plates, where there is no wire: no current passes through it, so the answer is zero.

    Same loop, two different answers. Something is missing.

    The displacement current#

    Maxwell's fix was to add a term involving the rate of change of the electric field:

    \[\curl{\vec{B}} = \mu_0\vec{J} + \mu_0\epsilon_0\pdv{\vec{E}}{t}\]

    The new piece, \( \vec{J}_d = \epsilon_0\pdv{\vec{E}}{t} \), is called the displacement current. No charge actually flows, so it isn't really a current, but it appears in the equation exactly as if it were one.

    Now taking the divergence gives zero on both sides, because

    \[\div{\vec{J}} + \epsilon_0\pdv{}{t}\left( \div{\vec{E}} \right) = \div{\vec{J}} + \pdv{\rho}{t} = 0\]

    by the continuity equation. The contradiction is gone. For the capacitor: between the plates, the electric field is growing as charge builds up, and its displacement current exactly makes up for the missing wire current. Both surfaces now give \( \mu_0 I \).

    Maxwell saw the huge consequence straight away. With this new term, a changing electric field makes a magnetic field, just as a changing magnetic field makes an electric field. So the two can keep creating each other, travelling through empty space with no charges or currents at all. That travelling disturbance is light. Chapter 9 works this out.

    Maxwell's equations#

    \[\div{\vec{E}} = \frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}, \qquad \curl{\vec{E}} = -\pdv{\vec{B}}{t}\] \[\div{\vec{B}} = 0, \qquad \curl{\vec{B}} = \mu_0\vec{J} + \mu_0\epsilon_0\pdv{\vec{E}}{t}\]

    In words:

    1. Electric field lines start and end on charges (Gauss).
    2. A changing magnetic field creates a swirling electric field (Faraday).
    3. There are no magnetic charges; magnetic field lines form closed loops.
    4. Currents and changing electric fields create swirling magnetic fields (Ampère–Maxwell).

    Together with the Lorentz force law \( \vec{F} = q(\vec{E} + \vec{v}\cross\vec{B}) \) and the continuity equation, these contain all of classical electrodynamics.

    Magnetic charge#

    The equations are lopsided: \( \vec{E} \) has a source (charge) and \( \vec{B} \) doesn't. If magnetic charge \( \rho_m \) and magnetic current \( \vec{J}_m \) existed, the equations would be symmetric:

    \[\div{\vec{B}} = \mu_0\rho_m, \qquad \curl{\vec{E}} = -\mu_0\vec{J}_m - \pdv{\vec{B}}{t}\]

    But no magnetic monopole has ever been found, so as far as we know \( \rho_m = \vec{J}_m = 0 \). The lopsidedness is a fact of experiment; nothing in the theory forbids monopoles.

    Maxwell's equations in matter#

    Split the charges and currents into free and bound parts, as in §4.3 and §6.3. (When the polarization changes with time, the moving bound charge makes a “polarization current”, which must be included too.) The result is

    \[\div{\vec{D}} = \rho_f, \qquad \curl{\vec{H}} = \vec{J}_f + \pdv{\vec{D}}{t}\]

    with the other two equations unchanged. For linear materials, \( \vec{D} = \epsilon\vec{E} \) and \( \vec{H} = \vec{B}/\mu \), which completes the set.

    Boundary conditions#

    At a boundary between two materials, with free surface charge \( \sigma_f \) and free surface current \( \vec{K}_f \):

    \[D_{\text{above}}^{\perp} - D_{\text{below}}^{\perp} = \sigma_f, \qquad B_{\text{above}}^{\perp} - B_{\text{below}}^{\perp} = 0\] \[\vec{E}_{\text{above}}^{\parallel} - \vec{E}_{\text{below}}^{\parallel} = \vec{0}, \qquad \vec{H}_{\text{above}}^{\parallel} - \vec{H}_{\text{below}}^{\parallel} = \vec{K}_f\cross\vu{n}\]

    With no free charge or current on the boundary, all four right-hand sides are zero. These conditions are what give the laws of reflection and refraction in Chapter 9.

    Example#

    A parallel-plate capacitor with circular plates of radius \( R \) is being charged by a steady current \( I \). Find the magnetic field between the plates at radius \( s < R \).

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    No real current flows between the plates, so the magnetic field there comes entirely from the displacement current.

    The field between the plates is \( E = \sigma/\epsilon_0 = Q/\epsilon_0\pi R^2 \), so

    \[\pdv{E}{t} = \frac{1}{\epsilon_0 \pi R^2}\dv{Q}{t} = \frac{I}{\epsilon_0\pi R^2}\]

    Use the corrected Ampère's law on a circle of radius \( s \) between the plates. The displacement current through it is

    \[I_d = \epsilon_0 \pdv{E}{t}\left( \pi s^2 \right) = I\frac{s^2}{R^2}\]

    so

    \[B(s)\,2\pi s = \mu_0 I \frac{s^2}{R^2} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \vec{B} = \frac{\mu_0 I s}{2\pi R^2}\vu{\phi}\]

    This is exactly the field inside a thick wire of radius \( R \) carrying current \( I \) (§5.3). The displacement current is spread evenly across the gap, just as a real current would be across a wire. At \( s = R \) it joins smoothly onto the outside field \( \mu_0 I/2\pi R \).

    Example#

    Show that Maxwell's equations in a region with no charges or currents admit solutions in which \( \vec{E} \) and \( \vec{B} \) satisfy a wave equation, and identify the propagation speed.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    With \( \rho = 0 \) and \( \vec{J} = \vec{0} \), take the curl of Faraday's law:

    \[\curl{(\curl{\vec{E}})} = \curl{\left( -\pdv{\vec{B}}{t} \right)} = -\pdv{}{t}\left( \curl{\vec{B}} \right)\]

    On the right, use Ampère's law with the displacement term. On the left, use the identity \( \curl{(\curl{\vec{E}})} = \grad(\div{\vec{E}}) - \laplacian\vec{E} \), with \( \div{\vec{E}} = 0 \):

    \[-\laplacian\vec{E} = -\mu_0\epsilon_0 \pdv{^2\vec{E}}{t^2}\]

    that is,

    \[\laplacian\vec{E} = \mu_0\epsilon_0\pdv{^2\vec{E}}{t^2}\]

    This is the wave equation, with speed

    \[v = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0}} = 3.00\times10^8\ \text{m/s}\]

    The same steps give the same equation for \( \vec{B} \).

    This is remarkable. The speed comes entirely from two constants measured in experiments with charges, wires and magnets, which had nothing to do with light. Yet it equals the measured speed of light. That's how Maxwell concluded that light is an electromagnetic wave. Chapter 9 develops this.

    Notice the displacement term is essential. Without it the right-hand side would be zero and there would be no waves.

    Full derivation: §7.3.
