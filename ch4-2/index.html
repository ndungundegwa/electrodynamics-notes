    4.2 The Field of a Polarized Object#

    Bound charges#

    If you know the polarization \( \vec{P} \), you can find the potential by adding up the potentials of all the tiny dipoles:

    \[V(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_V \frac{\vec{P}(\vec{r'})\cdot\vu{\gr}}{\gr^2}\dd{\tau'}\]

    Using \( \grad'(1/\gr) = \vu{\gr}/\gr^2 \) and integrating by parts turns this into two terms, each with a clear meaning:

    \[V(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\oint_S \frac{\vec{P}\cdot\vu{n}}{\gr}\dd{a'} + \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_V \frac{-\div{\vec{P}}}{\gr}\dd{\tau'}\]

    The first term is the potential of a surface charge, and the second of a volume charge, with

    \[\sigma_b \equiv \vec{P}\cdot\vu{n}, \qquad \rho_b \equiv -\div{\vec{P}}\]

    These are the bound charges. The big payoff: a polarized object produces exactly the same field as these two ordinary charge distributions. So everything from Chapters 2 and 3 applies directly.

    What the bound charges really are#

    Bound charges are real charge, not a mathematical trick.

    Surface charge. Picture a bar polarized evenly along its length, like a row of people each holding \( + \) in their right hand and \( - \) in their left. Inside, each person's \( + \) hand is next to their neighbour's \( - \) hand, and they cancel. Only the two people at the ends have an unpaired hand. So charge shows up on the end surfaces. If \( \vec{P} \) is at angle \( \theta \) to the surface, the charge per unit area is \( P\cos\theta = \vec{P}\cdot\vu{n} \).

    Volume charge. If the polarization is uneven, the cancellation inside isn't perfect. More charge gets pushed out of some region than pushed in, and a net charge is left behind. The divergence measures exactly this mismatch. The minus sign is there because positive divergence means positive charge is pushed out, leaving negative charge behind.

    The object is neutral overall, so the bound charges always add up to zero:

    \[\oint_S \sigma_b \dd{a} + \int_V \rho_b \dd{\tau} = \oint \vec{P}\cdot\dd{\vec{a}} - \int (\div{\vec{P}})\dd{\tau} = 0\]

    by the divergence theorem.

    Microscopic and macroscopic fields#

    Zoom right in to a dielectric and the true field is wild: enormous near each nucleus, pointing every which way between atoms. The \( \vec{E} \) we use is the average field, taken over a region big enough to contain many atoms but small compared with the object.

    Does averaging change the answer? No. Average the field over a small sphere holding many atoms. The charges outside the sphere contribute their field at its centre. The charges inside contribute \( -\vec{p}/4\pi\epsilon_0 R^3 \), which depends only on their total dipole moment. Put together, the average field is exactly the field of the bound charges above. The exact size of the averaging region doesn't matter.

    Example#

    Find the field of a uniformly polarized sphere of radius \( R \), with \( \vec{P} = P\vu{z} \), both inside and outside.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    The polarization is uniform, so \( \div{\vec{P}} = 0 \): no bound volume charge. The bound surface charge is

    \[\sigma_b = \vec{P}\cdot\vu{n} = P\cos\theta\]

    using \( \vu{n} = \vu{r} \) on the sphere. Positive on top, negative on the bottom.

    This is exactly the charge from Example 3.4-b, with \( k = P \). That problem gave a pure dipole potential outside, and (by the same method) a uniform field inside:

    \[V_{\text{in}} = \frac{P}{3\epsilon_0}r\cos\theta = \frac{P}{3\epsilon_0}z, \qquad V_{\text{out}} = \frac{PR^3}{3\epsilon_0}\frac{\cos\theta}{r^2}\]

    Take the gradient of the inside potential:

    \[\vec{E}_{\text{in}} = -\grad V_{\text{in}} = -\frac{P}{3\epsilon_0}\vu{z} = -\frac{\vec{P}}{3\epsilon_0}\]

    The field inside is uniform and points against the polarization. That makes sense: it runs from the positive charge on top to the negative charge on the bottom. It is called a depolarizing field, because it works against the polarization that created it.

    Outside, the field is that of a single dipole with moment

    \[\vec{p} = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 \vec{P}\]

    which is just polarization × volume, as you'd expect.

    Example#

    A sphere of radius \( R \) carries polarization \( \vec{P} = k\vec{r} \), with \( k \) constant. Find the bound charges and the field inside.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Volume charge. The polarization is uneven, so

    \[\rho_b = -\div{\vec{P}} = -k\,\div{\vec{r}} = -3k\]

    using \( \div{\vec{r}} = 3 \). So there is a uniform bound charge throughout the sphere.

    Surface charge. On the surface \( \vec{r} = R\vu{r} \), so

    \[\sigma_b = \vec{P}\cdot\vu{n} = kR\]

    Check: the total bound charge should be zero:

    \[(-3k)\left( \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 \right) + (kR)\left( 4\pi R^2 \right) = -4\pi k R^3 + 4\pi k R^3 = 0\]

    ✓

    Field inside. Use Gauss's law on a sphere of radius \( r < R \). It encloses only volume charge, \( Q_{\text{enc}} = -3k \cdot \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3 \):

    \[E(r)\,4\pi r^2 = \frac{-4\pi k r^3}{\epsilon_0} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \vec{E} = -\frac{k\vec{r}}{\epsilon_0} = -\frac{\vec{P}}{\epsilon_0}\]

    Outside, a Gaussian sphere encloses zero total charge, so the field outside is zero.

    Full derivation: §4.2.
