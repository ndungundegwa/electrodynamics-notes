    1.1 Vector Algebra#

    A scalar has only a size (temperature, mass, charge). A vector has a size and a direction (velocity, force, electric field). Strictly, a vector is not just “three numbers”: it is three numbers that change in a particular way when you rotate your axes. The last part of this section explains what that means.

    Vector operations#

    You add vectors component by component; the order doesn't matter. Picture it as placing arrows head to tail. Subtracting \( \vec{B} \) means adding \( \vec{B} \) reversed. Multiplying by a scalar \( a \) stretches the arrow by \( a \) without turning it (it flips round if \( a < 0 \)).

    There are two ways to multiply two vectors, and they answer different questions.

    Dot product gives a number, and the order doesn't matter:

    \[\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} = AB\cos\theta = A_x B_x + A_y B_y + A_z B_z\]

    Here \( \theta \) is the angle between the arrows when drawn tail to tail. The dot product asks how much of one vector points along the other. It is zero for perpendicular vectors, and \( \vec{A} \cdot \vec{A} = A^2 \). Work, flux and potential energy are all built from it, because each only cares about the part of a vector along some direction.

    Cross product gives a vector, and swapping the order flips its sign:

    \[\vec{A} \cross \vec{B} = AB\sin\theta \, \vu{n} = \begin{vmatrix} \vu{x} & \vu{y} & \vu{z} \\ A_x & A_y & A_z \\ B_x & B_y & B_z \end{vmatrix}\]

    So \( \vec{A} \cross \vec{B} = -\vec{B} \cross \vec{A} \) and \( \vec{A} \cross \vec{A} = \vec{0} \). The unit vector \( \vu{n} \) sticks out perpendicular to the plane of \( \vec{A} \) and \( \vec{B} \); the right-hand rule picks which way. The size of the cross product is the area of the parallelogram the two vectors make, so it is zero for parallel vectors. It asks how much the two vectors point away from each other.

    Triple products#

    The scalar triple product gives a number, and you can cycle the letters round without changing it:

    \[\vec{A} \cdot (\vec{B} \cross \vec{C}) = \vec{B} \cdot (\vec{C} \cross \vec{A}) = \vec{C} \cdot (\vec{A} \cross \vec{B})\]

    It is the volume of the slanted box (parallelepiped) built on the three vectors. That explains the cycling: it's the same box whichever face you call the base. Swapping any two vectors flips the sign. In components it is the determinant with the three vectors as rows.

    The vector triple product simplifies by the “BAC-CAB” rule:

    \[\vec{A} \cross (\vec{B} \cross \vec{C}) = \vec{B} (\vec{A} \cdot \vec{C}) - \vec{C} (\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B})\]

    You will use this a lot in radiation (Chapter 11), where the fields of an accelerating charge contain double cross products. The brackets matter, because the cross product is not associative:

    \[\vec{A} \cross (\vec{B} \cross \vec{C}) \neq (\vec{A} \cross \vec{B}) \cross \vec{C}\]

    Any longer product of vectors can be broken down into these two forms.

    Position, displacement and separation vectors#

    The position of a point, measured from the origin, is

    \[\vec{r} = x\vu{x} + y\vu{y} + z\vu{z}, \qquad r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}, \qquad \vu{r} = \frac{\vec{r}}{r}\]

    and a tiny step in any direction is \( \dd{\vec{l}} = \dd{x},\vu{x} + \dd{y},\vu{y} + \dd{z},\vu{z} \).

    In electrodynamics there are always two points to keep apart: where the charge is, and where you are measuring the field. The source point \( \vec{r'} \) is where the charge sits. The field point \( \vec{r} \) is where you are. The arrow from the charge to you is the separation vector:

    \[\gr \equiv \vec{r} - \vec{r'}, \qquad \gr = |\vec{r} - \vec{r'}|, \qquad \vu{\gr} = \frac{\vec{r} - \vec{r'}}{|\vec{r} - \vec{r'}|}\]

    \( \gr \) is not \( \vec{r} \). Also, \( \vu{\gr} \) is not a constant: its direction changes as you move from one bit of charge to the next, so you cannot pull it outside an integral over the charge. Split it into Cartesian components first (\( \vu{x}, \vu{y}, \vu{z} \) are constant and can come out), then integrate.

    How vectors transform#

    If you rotate the coordinate axes, the components of a vector change according to

    \[\bar{A}_i = \sum_{j=1}^{3} R_{ij} A_j\]

    where \( R_{ij} \) is the rotation matrix. For example, rotating by angle \( \phi \) about the \( x \)-axis gives

    \[\begin{pmatrix} \bar{A}_y \\ \bar{A}_z \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\phi & \sin\phi \\ -\sin\phi & \cos\phi \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} A_y \\ A_z \end{pmatrix}\]

    This rule is what defines a vector. Three numbers that don't change this way are not a vector, even if you write them with arrows. The arrow itself stays the same when you turn your head, so its length \( A^2 = \vec{A} \cdot \vec{A} \) does not change under rotation. That is why quantities built from dot products (energy, work) mean the same thing in every set of axes.

    Example#

    Find the angle between the face diagonals of a cube.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Put one corner of a unit cube at the origin, with its edges along the axes. Two face diagonals starting at the origin are

    \[\vec{A} = 1\vu{x} + 0\vu{y} + 1\vu{z}, \qquad \vec{B} = 0\vu{x} + 1\vu{y} + 1\vu{z}\]

    Work out the dot product from the components:

    \[\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} = (1)(0) + (0)(1) + (1)(1) = 1\]

    and from the geometry, using \( A = B = \sqrt{2} \):

    \[\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B} = AB\cos\theta = 2\cos\theta\]

    Setting the two equal gives \( \cos\theta = 1/2 \), so \( \theta = 60^\circ \).

    This is the standard trick for finding angles: work out the dot product both ways and set them equal.

    Example#

    Find the area of the triangle with vertices at \( (1,0,0) \), \( (0,2,0) \) and \( (0,0,3) \), and a unit vector normal to its plane.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Take two edges of the triangle, both starting from the first vertex:

    \[\vec{A} = -\vu{x} + 2\vu{y}, \qquad \vec{B} = -\vu{x} + 3\vu{z}\]

    Their cross product is

    \[\vec{A} \cross \vec{B} = \begin{vmatrix} \vu{x} & \vu{y} & \vu{z} \\ -1 & 2 & 0 \\ -1 & 0 & 3 \end{vmatrix} = 6\vu{x} + 3\vu{y} + 2\vu{z}\]

    Its size is the area of the parallelogram on the two edges. The triangle is half of that parallelogram:

    \[|\vec{A} \cross \vec{B}| = \sqrt{36 + 9 + 4} = 7, \qquad \text{area} = \frac{7}{2}\]

    A cross product is always perpendicular to both vectors, so it is normal to the triangle's plane:

    \[\vu{n} = \frac{1}{7}\left( 6\vu{x} + 3\vu{y} + 2\vu{z} \right)\]

    The negative of this is just as good; taking the edges in the other order gives it.
