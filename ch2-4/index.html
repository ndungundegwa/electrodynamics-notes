    2.4 Work and Energy in Electrostatics#

    Work to move a charge#

    To move a test charge \( Q \) slowly from \( a \) to \( b \), you must push against the electric force. The work you do is

    \[W = -Q\int_a^b \vec{E}\cdot\dd{\vec{l}} = Q\left[ V(b) - V(a) \right]\]

    and it's the same for every path, because \( \curl{\vec{E}} = \vec{0} \). To bring a charge in from infinity, where \( V(\infty) = 0 \),

    \[W = QV(\vec{r})\]

    This is what potential means: \( V \) at a point is the work needed per coulomb to bring a charge there from far away.

    Energy of a group of point charges#

    Build up a group of charges one at a time. The first is free, since there is nothing yet to push against. Each new one costs work against the charges already there. Add up all the pairs:

    \[W = \frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j \neq i} \frac{q_i q_j}{\gr_{ij}} = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{n} q_i V(\vec{r}_i)\]

    Here \( V(\vec{r}_i) \) is the potential at charge \( i \) from all the other charges. The \( \frac{1}{2} \) is there because the double sum counts every pair twice (\( i,j \) and \( j,i \)). The \( j \neq i \) leaves out each charge's interaction with itself, which would be infinite for a point charge.

    Energy of a continuous distribution#

    For spread-out charge the sum becomes an integral:

    \[W = \frac{1}{2}\int \rho V \dd{\tau}\]

    Replace \( \rho \) by \( \epsilon_0 \div{\vec{E}} \), integrate by parts, and use the divergence theorem. The result is an integral over all space:

    \[W = \frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int_{\text{all space}} E^2 \dd{\tau}\]

    (The surface term disappears because far away \( V \) falls as \( 1/r \) and \( E \) as \( 1/r^2 \), faster than the surface area grows.)

    The two formulas give the same number, but they tell different stories about where the energy is. The first puts it on the charges. The second puts it in the field, with energy density \( \epsilon_0 E^2/2 \) (joules per cubic metre). In electrostatics it doesn't matter which story you believe. In Chapter 8 it matters a lot: light carries energy through empty space where there are no charges at all, so the energy really is in the field.

    Notice that \( E^2 \) is never negative, so the field formula always gives a positive energy. But \( \frac{1}{2}\sum q_i V_i \) is negative for a pair of opposite charges. The difference is the self-energy of the point charges that the sum left out, which is infinite.

    Energies don't simply add#

    Energy depends on \( E^2 \), so if you combine two charge distributions, the total energy is not just the sum of their separate energies:

    \[W_{\text{tot}} = \frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int |\vec{E}_1 + \vec{E}_2|^2 \dd{\tau} = W_1 + W_2 + \epsilon_0\int \vec{E}_1\cdot\vec{E}_2 \dd{\tau}\]

    The extra cross term is the interaction energy between the two distributions.

    Example#

    Find the energy of a uniformly charged spherical shell of radius \( R \) and total charge \( q \), by both available methods.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Method 1: charge × potential. On the shell the potential is \( V = q/4\pi\epsilon_0 R \), the same everywhere, so it comes out of the integral:

    \[W = \frac{1}{2}\int \sigma V \dd{a} = \frac{1}{2}\frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 R}\int \sigma \dd{a} = \frac{1}{8\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q^2}{R}\]

    Method 2: the field. Inside the shell \( \vec{E} = \vec{0} \); outside, \( E = q/4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2 \). Only the outside contributes:

    \[W = \frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int_R^\infty \left( \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2} \right)^2 4\pi r^2 \dd{r} = \frac{q^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0}\int_R^\infty \frac{\dd{r}}{r^2} = \frac{1}{8\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q^2}{R}\]

    Both methods agree. Notice the energy blows up as \( R \to 0 \): squeezing the charge into a point takes infinite energy. That is the infinite self-energy of a point charge, showing up openly.

    Example#

    Find the energy of a solid sphere of radius \( R \) carrying total charge \( q \) distributed uniformly through its volume.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Use the field method. This time the field inside is not zero. From §2.2,

    \[E_{\text{in}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q r}{R^3} \quad (r<R), \qquad E_{\text{out}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2} \quad (r>R)\]

    The outside part is exactly the same as for the shell above: \( q^2/8\pi\epsilon_0 R \). The inside adds

    \[\frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int_0^R \left( \frac{qr}{4\pi\epsilon_0 R^3} \right)^2 4\pi r^2 \dd{r} = \frac{q^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0 R^6}\int_0^R r^4 \dd{r} = \frac{q^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{1}{5R}\]

    Adding the two,

    \[W = \frac{q^2}{8\pi\epsilon_0 R}\left( 1 + \frac{1}{5} \right) = \frac{3}{5}\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q^2}{R}\]

    The solid sphere stores \( 6/5 \) times as much energy as the shell. The extra comes from the field inside the ball, which the shell doesn't have.

    Full derivation: §2.4.
