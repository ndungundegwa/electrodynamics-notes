    2.2 Divergence and Curl of Electrostatic Fields#

    The Helmholtz theorem (§1.6) says a field is fixed once you know its divergence and its curl. This section finds both for \( \vec{E} \).

    Field lines and flux#

    Field lines are drawn so that at every point they run along \( \vec{E} \), and they are packed closer together where the field is stronger. They start on positive charges and end on negative ones. They never cross, because the field can only point one way at any point.

    The flux through a surface,

    \[\Phi_E = \int_S \vec{E} \cdot \dd{\vec{a}}\]

    counts how many field lines pass through it (lines going out count positive, lines coming in count negative). For a point charge at the centre of a sphere of radius \( r \),

    \[\oint \vec{E} \cdot \dd{\vec{a}} = \int \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2}\left( r^2\sin\theta \dd{\theta}\dd{\phi} \right) = \frac{q}{\epsilon_0}\]

    The radius cancels. That makes sense: every field line leaving the charge must cross the sphere, however big it is. The same is true for any closed surface of any shape around the charge. And by superposition it works for any number of charges.

    Gauss's law#

    \[\oint_S \vec{E} \cdot \dd{\vec{a}} = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\epsilon_0}\]

    The total flux out of any closed surface equals the charge inside, divided by \( \epsilon_0 \). Charges outside don't count: their field lines go in one side and out the other. Using the divergence theorem on the left side, and writing \( Q_{\text{enc}} = \int \rho \dd{\tau} \), gives the differential form:

    \[\div{\vec{E}} = \frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}\]

    This is the first of Maxwell's equations. It says field lines spread out from wherever there is charge.

    Using Gauss's law#

    Gauss's law is always true, but it only lets you find \( \vec{E} \) easily when symmetry makes \( \vec{E} \) the same size everywhere on a surface and pointing straight through it. Then \( \oint \vec{E} \cdot \dd{\vec{a}} \) is just \( E \times \text{area} \). This works for three shapes:

    • Spherical symmetry: use a sphere with the same centre
    • Cylindrical symmetry: use a cylinder with the same axis
    • Plane symmetry: use a flat “pillbox” poking through the plane

    The standard results:

    \[\vec{E}_{\text{sphere}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2}\vu{r}, \qquad \vec{E}_{\text{line}} = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi\epsilon_0 s}\vu{s}, \qquad \vec{E}_{\text{plane}} = \frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}\vu{n}\]

    Notice that the field of an infinite plane doesn't get weaker with distance.

    The curl of E#

    Take a single point charge at the origin and work out the line integral between two points. The field is radial, so \( \vec{E} \cdot \dd{\vec{l}} = E_r \dd{r} \) (only movement towards or away from the charge counts):

    \[\int_a^b \vec{E} \cdot \dd{\vec{l}} = \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_{r_a}^{r_b}\frac{\dd{r}}{r^2} = \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left( \frac{1}{r_a} - \frac{1}{r_b} \right)\]

    The answer depends only on the start and end points, not the path. So around any closed loop \( \oint \vec{E} \cdot \dd{\vec{l}} = 0 \), and by Stokes' theorem

    \[\curl{\vec{E}} = \vec{0}\]

    By superposition this holds for any arrangement of charges that are not moving. So static electric fields never swirl. This stops being true once fields change with time (Chapter 7).

    Because the curl is zero, §1.6 says \( \vec{E} \) can be written using a scalar potential. That is §2.3.

    Example#

    Find the field inside and outside a solid sphere of radius \( R \) carrying uniform volume charge density \( \rho \).

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    The charge is spherically symmetric, so the field points radially and its size depends only on \( r \). Use a sphere of radius \( r \) with the same centre as the Gaussian surface. On it, \( \vec{E} \) points straight out and has the same size everywhere, so

    \[\oint \vec{E} \cdot \dd{\vec{a}} = E(r)\, 4\pi r^2 = \frac{Q_{\text{enc}}}{\epsilon_0}\]

    Outside (\( r > R \)), the surface encloses all the charge, \( Q_{\text{enc}} = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 \rho \), so

    \[\vec{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{Q}{r^2}\vu{r}\]

    exactly as if all the charge were a point at the centre.

    Inside (\( r < R \)), only the charge within radius \( r \) is enclosed, \( Q_{\text{enc}} = \frac{4}{3}\pi r^3\rho \), so

    \[E(r)\,4\pi r^2 = \frac{4\pi r^3 \rho}{3\epsilon_0} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \vec{E} = \frac{\rho}{3\epsilon_0} r\,\vu{r}\]

    So the field rises steadily from zero at the centre, is strongest at the surface, and then falls off as \( 1/r^2 \) outside. The two formulas give the same value at \( r = R \), as they must, since there's no extra charge sitting on the surface.

    Example#

    Two infinite parallel planes carry uniform surface charge densities \( +\sigma \) and \( -\sigma \). Find the field in all three regions.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Each plane on its own makes a field of size \( \sigma/2\epsilon_0 \), the same at any distance. It points away from the positive plane and towards the negative one. Now add the two.

    Between the planes, both fields point from the positive plane towards the negative one, so they add:

    \[E = \frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0} + \frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0} = \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0}\]

    Outside, on either side, the positive plane's field points away from it and the negative plane's field points towards it. They are equal and opposite, so

    \[E = 0\]

    All the field is trapped between the plates. This is how a parallel-plate capacitor works (§2.5). It relies on the planes being infinite; near the edges of real plates some field leaks out.

    Full derivation: §2.2.
