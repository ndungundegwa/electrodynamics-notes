    12.2 Relativistic Mechanics#

    Proper time and proper velocity#

    Ordinary velocity \( \dd{\vec{l}}/\dd{t} \) is awkward in relativity. The distance is measured by the ground observer, but the particle's own clock would give a different time, so it's a mix of two viewpoints and changes in a messy way between frames. Instead, divide by the proper time \( \bar{t} \), the time on a clock carried along with the particle. That gives the proper velocity

    \[\vec{\eta} \equiv \dv{\vec{l}}{\bar{t}} = \gamma\vec{u}\]

    Everyone agrees on proper time, so proper velocity transforms in the same simple way as position. It's part of a proper four-vector, which is why it's the better choice.

    Relativistic energy and momentum#

    \[\vec{p} = \gamma m\vec{u} = \frac{m\vec{u}}{\sqrt{1-u^2/c^2}}, \qquad E = \gamma mc^2 = \frac{mc^2}{\sqrt{1-u^2/c^2}}\]

    Here \( m \) is the rest mass, the same for all observers. For a particle at rest,

    \[E = mc^2\]

    the famous rest energy, which a particle has just by existing. At low speeds (\( u \ll c \)),

    \[E \approx mc^2 + \frac{1}{2}mu^2 + \cdots\]

    so the ordinary kinetic energy \( \frac{1}{2}mu^2 \) appears as the first extra piece on top of the rest energy.

    Energy and momentum together make a four-vector \( p^\mu = (E/c, \vec{p}) \), and its “length” gives

    \[E^2 - p^2c^2 = m^2c^4\]

    which is the same in every frame. This is the most useful formula in the section. For a massless particle it gives \( E = pc \). That's how light can carry momentum without having mass, which is why radiation pressure (§8.2) exists.

    You may see “relativistic mass” \( \gamma m \) in older books. Modern physics doesn't use it. \( m \) always means rest mass, and the \( \gamma \) goes into the momentum and energy.

    Collisions#

    In collisions, energy and momentum are conserved; mass is not. The total rest mass after a collision can differ from before; the difference turns into kinetic energy, or comes from it. This is where nuclear energy comes from.

    The standard trick: use \( E^2 - p^2c^2 \), which is the same in every frame. Calculate it in whichever frame is easiest (often the frame where the total momentum is zero), then use it in the lab frame.

    Forces#

    Newton's second law survives as

    \[\vec{F} = \dv{\vec{p}}{t}\]

    using the relativistic momentum. But \( \vec{F} = m\vec{a} \) does not survive. Because \( \gamma \) depends on speed, force and acceleration generally don't even point the same way.

    A constant force does not give constant acceleration. The speed creeps closer and closer to \( c \) but never reaches it, while the momentum keeps growing without limit. That's why no accelerator, however powerful, can push a particle to \( c \).

    The Lorentz force law \( \vec{F} = q(\vec{E} + \vec{u}\cross\vec{B}) \) needs no change at all. It was already correct for relativity, and §12.3 shows that's no coincidence.

    Example#

    A particle of rest mass \( m \) is accelerated through a potential difference until its total energy is three times its rest energy. Find its speed and momentum.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    \( E = 3mc^2 \) and \( E = \gamma mc^2 \), so straight away \( \gamma = 3 \). Solve for the speed:

    \[\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-u^2/c^2}} = 3 \quad \Rightarrow \quad 1 - \frac{u^2}{c^2} = \frac{1}{9}\]

    so \( u^2/c^2 = 8/9 \) and

    \[u = c\sqrt{\frac{8}{9}} = \frac{2\sqrt{2}}{3}c \approx 0.943c\]

    For momentum, the quickest route is \( E^2 - p^2c^2 = m^2c^4 \):

    \[E^2 - p^2c^2 = m^2c^4 \quad \Rightarrow \quad p^2c^2 = (3mc^2)^2 - (mc^2)^2 = 8m^2c^4\]

    so \( p = 2\sqrt{2},mc \approx 2.83mc \).

    The kinetic energy is \( E - mc^2 = 2mc^2 \), twice the rest energy. Notice that tripling the energy only gets the speed to \( 0.943c \). Adding more energy from here mostly adds momentum; the speed hardly changes.

    Example#

    A pion at rest decays into two photons. Find the energy of each. Then consider a pion moving at \( 0.6c \) decaying into two photons emitted along the direction of motion.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    At rest. The momentum starts at zero, so the photons must go in opposite directions with equal momentum, and so equal energy. Energy conservation gives

    \[2E_\gamma = m_\pi c^2 \quad \Rightarrow \quad E_\gamma = \frac{m_\pi c^2}{2}\]

    Each photon gets half the pion's rest energy.

    Moving. \( \gamma = 1/\sqrt{1-0.36} = 1.25 \), so the pion has

    \[E = 1.25\,m_\pi c^2, \qquad p = \gamma m_\pi u = 1.25\,m_\pi(0.6c) = 0.75\,m_\pi c\]

    Call the forward photon's energy \( E_1 \) and the backward one's \( E_2 \). For photons \( E = pc \), so conservation of energy and momentum gives

    \[E_1 + E_2 = 1.25\,m_\pi c^2, \qquad \frac{E_1 - E_2}{c} = 0.75\,m_\pi c\]

    Adding and subtracting:

    \[E_1 = m_\pi c^2, \qquad E_2 = 0.25\,m_\pi c^2\]

    The forward photon has four times the energy of the backward one. This is the relativistic Doppler effect. Also notice: the pion had rest mass, but the photons each have zero rest mass. Mass isn't conserved; energy and momentum are.
