    3.1 Laplace's Equation#

    The problem#

    To find \( V \) by integrating over the charge, you need to know where the charge is. Usually you don't. On a conductor, the charge moves around in response to everything nearby, so where it ends up is part of the answer, not part of the question.

    So instead we solve the differential equation directly. Where there is no charge, Poisson's equation becomes Laplace's equation:

    \[\laplacian V = \pdv{^2 V}{x^2} + \pdv{^2 V}{y^2} + \pdv{^2 V}{z^2} = 0\]

    together with conditions on the boundary (for example, “this conductor is at 10 V”). Solutions of Laplace's equation are called harmonic functions.

    One dimension#

    If \( V \) depends only on \( x \), then \( \dd{^2 V}/\dd{x^2} = 0 \), so

    \[V(x) = mx + b\]

    a straight line, pinned down by the values at the two ends. Even this simple case has two features that carry over to three dimensions:

    • \( V(x) \) is the average of the values an equal distance either side: \( V(x) = \frac{1}{2}\left[ V(x+a) + V(x-a) \right] \).
    • There are no bumps or dips in the middle. The largest and smallest values are at the ends.

    Three dimensions#

    Both features survive.

    Average value property. The potential at any point equals its average over any sphere centred on that point:

    \[V(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi R^2}\oint_{\text{sphere}} V \dd{a}\]

    No bumps or dips. \( V \) takes its highest and lowest values on the boundary, never inside. A peak inside would be higher than everything around it, so higher than its own average, which the property above forbids.

    That second fact is Earnshaw's theorem, and it has a real consequence: you cannot trap a charge in a stable position using electrostatic forces alone. A stable trap needs a dip in potential energy, and there are no dips in empty space.

    A good picture: a soap film stretched across a bent wire frame. The film takes the smoothest shape that fits the frame, with no bumps of its own. Solutions of Laplace's equation behave the same way.

    Boundary conditions and uniqueness#

    First uniqueness theorem. If you know \( V \) everywhere on the boundary of a region, there is only one solution of Laplace's equation inside.

    Proof idea: suppose there were two solutions, \( V_1 \) and \( V_2 \). Their difference \( V_3 = V_1 - V_2 \) also satisfies Laplace's equation, and it is zero all over the boundary. It can't have a peak or dip inside, so it must be zero everywhere. The two solutions are the same.

    The theorem also works for Poisson's equation (with charge inside), and for regions with inner as well as outer boundaries.

    Why this matters: you are allowed to guess. If you find a solution by any method at all (inspection, symmetry, a clever trick like images in §3.2), and it satisfies the equation and the boundary conditions, then it is the answer.

    Second uniqueness theorem. In a region surrounded by conductors, with known charge density inside, the field is fixed if you know the total charge on each conductor. You don't need to know their potentials.

    Example#

    Show that the average potential over a spherical surface of radius \( R \) due to a point charge \( q \) lying outside the sphere equals the potential at the centre.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Put the sphere's centre at the origin and the charge on the \( z \)-axis at distance \( z > R \). By the cosine rule, a point on the sphere at angle \( \theta \) is a distance

    \[\gr = \sqrt{R^2 + z^2 - 2Rz\cos\theta}\]

    from the charge. The average over the sphere is

    \[V_{\text{avg}} = \frac{1}{4\pi R^2}\oint \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q}{\gr}\dd{a} = \frac{q}{16\pi^2\epsilon_0 R^2}\int \frac{R^2\sin\theta \dd{\theta}\dd{\phi}}{\sqrt{R^2+z^2-2Rz\cos\theta}}\]

    The \( \phi \) integral gives \( 2\pi \). For \( \theta \), substitute \( u = \cos\theta \):

    \[V_{\text{avg}} = \frac{q}{8\pi\epsilon_0}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\dd{u}}{\sqrt{R^2+z^2-2Rzu}} = \frac{q}{8\pi\epsilon_0}\left[ \frac{-\sqrt{R^2+z^2-2Rzu}}{Rz} \right]_{-1}^{1}\]

    At the limits the square roots are \( |z - R| = z-R \) and \( z+R \):

    \[V_{\text{avg}} = \frac{q}{8\pi\epsilon_0 Rz}\left[ (z+R) - (z-R) \right] = \frac{q}{8\pi\epsilon_0 Rz}(2R) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q}{z}\]

    That is exactly the potential at the centre, a distance \( z \) from the charge. Any charge outside the sphere is a sum of point charges, so the result holds for all of them. This proves the average value property.

    Example#

    A region contains no charge and is bounded by a surface held entirely at potential \( V_0 \). Determine the potential and field inside.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Try the simplest guess: \( V = V_0 \) everywhere inside. A constant has all second derivatives zero, so it satisfies Laplace's equation. And it matches the boundary, where \( V = V_0 \).

    By the first uniqueness theorem, that guess is the only solution. So \( V = V_0 \) everywhere inside, and

    \[\vec{E} = -\grad V = \vec{0}\]

    This is the shielding result of §2.5 again: an empty hollow inside a conductor has no field, because the conductor's surface is all at one potential. No calculation was needed, just a guess plus the uniqueness theorem. That is typical of how the theorem is used.

    Full derivation: §3.1.
