    6.1 Magnetization#

    Three kinds of magnetic material#

    Every material responds a little to a magnetic field by becoming magnetized. There are three types of response:

    • Paramagnets (e.g. aluminium, oxygen) become magnetized along the field, and are weakly pulled towards a magnet.
    • Diamagnets (e.g. water, copper, bismuth) become magnetized against the field, and are weakly pushed away. In fact every material is diamagnetic; it just goes unnoticed when a stronger effect is also present.
    • Ferromagnets (e.g. iron, nickel) stay magnetized after the field is removed. The effect is far stronger than the other two. This is what fridge magnets are.

    In every case the cause is the tiny magnetic dipoles of atoms, which come from the electrons' orbits and from electron spin.

    Twists and forces on magnetic dipoles#

    A current loop in a uniform field feels no net force (§5.1), but it does get twisted:

    \[\vec{N} = \vec{m}\cross\vec{B}\]

    This turns \( \vec{m} \) to line up with \( \vec{B} \), like a compass needle. That is how paramagnetism works, exactly like polar molecules lining up in §4.1. In most atoms, electrons pair up with opposite spins and their moments cancel. So paramagnetism usually needs an unpaired electron, typically from an odd number of electrons.

    In a non-uniform field there is also a net force:

    \[\vec{F} = \grad\left( \vec{m}\cdot\vec{B} \right)\]

    Where diamagnetism comes from#

    Diamagnetism works differently, and happens even in atoms with no magnetic moment of their own. Picture an electron orbiting in a circle, which is a tiny current loop. Now switch on a field at right angles to the orbit. By Faraday's law (Chapter 7), a changing magnetic field creates an electric field, and that nudges the electron's speed up or down.

    Changing the speed changes the loop's dipole moment. Working it through, the change in \( \vec{m} \) always points against the applied field, whichever way the electron was going round:

    \[\Delta\vec{m} = -\frac{e^2 R^2}{4m_e}\vec{B}\]

    So the material is pushed away from the magnet. Because this needs no existing moment, it happens in every material. But it's much weaker than paramagnetism, so you only notice it when paramagnetism is absent.

    Magnetization#

    The large-scale description works just like §4.1. The magnetization is

    \[\vec{M} \equiv \text{magnetic dipole moment per unit volume}\]

    with units A/m. Whatever happens inside the atoms, \( \vec{M} \) is all we need from now on. §6.2 works out the field a magnetized object produces.

    Example#

    A square current loop of side \( a \) carrying current \( I \) is free to rotate about an axis in its plane, in a uniform field \( \vec{B} \) at angle \( \theta \) to the loop's normal. Find the torque and the energy.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    The dipole moment is \( m = Ia^2 \), pointing along the loop's normal (right-hand rule). The torque is

    \[N = mB\sin\theta = Ia^2 B\sin\theta\]

    and it acts to make \( \theta \) smaller.

    You can check this from the forces on the four sides. The field is perpendicular to the rotation axis. The two sides parallel to the axis each feel a force \( F = IaB \), in opposite directions. These forces are offset by a lever arm \( a\sin\theta \), giving \( N = IaB \cdot a\sin\theta \), as above. The other two sides feel equal and opposite forces along the same line (along the axis), so they cancel completely and give no twist.

    The energy comes from integrating the torque, just as for the electric dipole in §4.1:

    \[U = -\vec{m}\cdot\vec{B} = -Ia^2 B\cos\theta\]

    It is lowest at \( \theta = 0 \), with the loop's normal along \( \vec{B} \). That is the stable position. Atomic “loops” settling into this position is paramagnetism.

    Example#

    Estimate the magnetic dipole moment of an electron in a circular orbit of radius \( R \) at speed \( v \), and express it in terms of the orbital angular momentum.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    The electron goes round once in time \( T = 2\pi R/v \), so it acts as a current

    \[I = \frac{e}{T} = \frac{ev}{2\pi R}\]

    The dipole moment is current × area:

    \[m = I\pi R^2 = \frac{ev}{2\pi R}\pi R^2 = \frac{evR}{2}\]

    The orbital angular momentum is \( L = m_e v R \), so

    \[m = \frac{e}{2m_e}L\]

    Magnetic moment is proportional to angular momentum. The constant \( e/2m_e \) is called the gyromagnetic ratio. The electron is negative, so \( \vec{m} \) points opposite to \( \vec{L} \).

    This link explains why magnetism always goes with spinning or orbiting charge, and why electron spin (angular momentum without any actual orbit) gives a magnetic moment. The classical picture gets the right size, but spin is really a quantum effect, so treating it classically, as we do for materials, is a bit of a fudge (Griffiths admits as much).

    Full derivation: §6.1.
