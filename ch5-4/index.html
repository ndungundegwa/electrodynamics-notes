    5.4 Magnetic Vector Potential#

    The vector potential#

    \( \div{\vec{B}} = 0 \) always, so by §1.6 we can write \( \vec{B} \) as the curl of something:

    \[\vec{B} = \curl{\vec{A}}\]

    \( \vec{A} \) is the magnetic vector potential. It plays the role that \( V \) plays for \( \vec{E} = -\grad V \). The saving is smaller, though: \( V \) replaced three components with one number, but \( \vec{A} \) still has three components.

    Gauge freedom and the Coulomb gauge#

    \( \vec{A} \) is not unique. Add the gradient of any function and \( \vec{B} \) doesn't change, because the curl of a gradient is zero:

    \[\vec{A}' = \vec{A} + \grad \lambda \quad \Rightarrow \quad \curl{\vec{A}'} = \curl{\vec{A}}\]

    We can use this freedom to make \( \vec{A} \) as convenient as possible. The usual choice in magnetostatics is the Coulomb gauge:

    \[\div{\vec{A}} = 0\]

    You can always arrange this. Starting from any \( \vec{A} \), pick \( \lambda \) to solve \( \laplacian\lambda = -\div{\vec{A}} \). That is just Poisson's equation, which always has a solution.

    Poisson's equation for A#

    Put \( \vec{B} = \curl{\vec{A}} \) into Ampère's law and use \( \curl{(\curl{\vec{A}})} = \grad(\div{\vec{A}}) - \laplacian\vec{A} \). In the Coulomb gauge the first term is zero, leaving

    \[\laplacian \vec{A} = -\mu_0 \vec{J}\]

    That's three copies of Poisson's equation, one for each component. We already know how to solve Poisson's equation from electrostatics (\( \laplacian V = -\rho/\epsilon_0 \) gives \( V = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int \rho/\gr \)), so just copy the answer:

    \[\vec{A}(\vec{r}) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int \frac{\vec{J}(\vec{r'})}{\gr}\dd{\tau'}\]

    or \( \vec{A} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\int \dd{\vec{l'}}/\gr \) for a wire. There's no cross product, and \( \gr \) instead of \( \gr^2 \), so this is usually much easier than Biot-Savart. Handy picture: \( \vec{A} \) tends to point the same way as the current that makes it.

    Boundary conditions#

    Crossing a sheet of surface current \( \vec{K} \):

    \[\vec{B}_{\text{above}}^{\perp} = \vec{B}_{\text{below}}^{\perp}, \qquad \vec{B}_{\text{above}}^{\parallel} - \vec{B}_{\text{below}}^{\parallel} = \mu_0 \vec{K}\cross\vu{n}\]

    The perpendicular part doesn't jump (because \( \div{\vec{B}} = 0 \)). The parallel part does (because of Ampère's law). This is the opposite of \( \vec{E} \), where the perpendicular part jumps and the parallel part doesn't.

    \( \vec{A} \) itself doesn't jump, but its slope does:

    \[\vec{A}_{\text{above}} = \vec{A}_{\text{below}}, \qquad \pdv{\vec{A}_{\text{above}}}{n} - \pdv{\vec{A}_{\text{below}}}{n} = -\mu_0\vec{K}\]

    Multipole expansion and the magnetic dipole#

    Expanding \( 1/\gr \) as in §3.4 gives a multipole expansion for \( \vec{A} \). The monopole term is always zero for a closed current loop, because \( \oint \dd{\vec{l'}} = 0 \). That's the “no magnetic monopoles” rule showing up again.

    So the first term is the dipole:

    \[\vec{A}_{\text{dip}}(\vec{r}) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\frac{\vec{m}\cross\vu{r}}{r^2}\]

    with the magnetic dipole moment

    \[\vec{m} \equiv I\int \dd{\vec{a}} = I\vec{a}\]

    which is current × area of the loop, pointing perpendicular to the loop (right-hand rule: fingers along the current, thumb along \( \vec{m} \)). Taking the curl gives a field with exactly the same shape as the electric dipole field of §3.4:

    \[\vec{B}_{\text{dip}} = \frac{\mu_0 m}{4\pi r^3}\left( 2\cos\theta\,\vu{r} + \sin\theta\,\vu{\theta} \right)\]

    There is never a monopole term, so far from any small current distribution the dipole term wins. (In electrostatics, any net charge would drown out the dipole.)

    Example#

    Find the vector potential of an infinite solenoid with \( n \) turns per unit length, radius \( R \), carrying current \( I \).

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    The integral is awkward here, so use a trick. Apply Stokes' theorem to \( \vec{B} = \curl{\vec{A}} \), over a disc of radius \( s \) centred on the solenoid's axis:

    \[\oint \vec{A}\cdot\dd{\vec{l}} = \int (\curl{\vec{A}})\cdot\dd{\vec{a}} = \int \vec{B}\cdot\dd{\vec{a}} = \Phi\]

    The line integral of \( \vec{A} \) round a loop equals the magnetic flux through the loop. It's just like Ampère's law, with \( \vec{A} \) in place of \( \vec{B} \) and flux in place of current.

    By symmetry \( \vec{A} \) circles the axis and depends only on \( s \), so the left side is \( A(s),2\pi s \).

    Inside (\( s < R \)), the field is uniform, \( \mu_0 n I \), so the flux is \( \mu_0 n I \pi s^2 \):

    \[A(s)\,2\pi s = \mu_0 n I \pi s^2 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \vec{A} = \frac{\mu_0 n I}{2}s\,\vu{\phi}\]

    Outside (\( s > R \)), there's no field beyond \( R \), so the flux stops growing at \( \mu_0 n I \pi R^2 \):

    \[\vec{A} = \frac{\mu_0 n I R^2}{2s}\vu{\phi}\]

    Notice: \( \vec{A} \) is not zero outside the solenoid, even though \( \vec{B} \) is. This is more than a mathematical curiosity. In the Aharonov-Bohm effect (a quantum effect), electrons that travel only through the field-free region outside a solenoid are still affected by \( \vec{A} \).

    Example#

    A spherical shell of radius \( R \) carries uniform surface charge \( \sigma \) and rotates about the \( z \)-axis at angular velocity \( \omega \). Find its magnetic dipole moment.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Slice the shell into thin rings at angle \( \theta \), each of angular width \( \dd{\theta} \). A ring has radius \( R\sin\theta \), area \( \dd{a} = 2\pi R^2\sin\theta \dd{\theta} \), and so charge \( \dd{q} = \sigma 2\pi R^2 \sin\theta \dd{\theta} \).

    Each ring goes round once every \( T = 2\pi/\omega \), so it is a current

    \[\dd{I} = \frac{\dd{q}}{T} = \frac{\omega}{2\pi}\sigma 2\pi R^2 \sin\theta \dd{\theta} = \sigma \omega R^2 \sin\theta \dd{\theta}\]

    Its dipole moment is current × the area it encloses, \( \pi (R\sin\theta)^2 \):

    \[\dd{m} = \pi R^2 \sin^2\theta \cdot \sigma\omega R^2 \sin\theta \dd{\theta} = \pi \sigma \omega R^4 \sin^3\theta \dd{\theta}\]

    Add up all the rings, using \( \int_0^\pi \sin^3\theta \dd{\theta} = 4/3 \):

    \[m = \pi\sigma\omega R^4 \cdot \frac{4}{3} = \frac{4\pi}{3}\sigma\omega R^4\]

    pointing along \( \vu{z} \). Every ring spins the same way, so all the contributions add; none cancel.

    Full derivation: §5.4.
