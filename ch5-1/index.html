    5.1 The Lorentz Force Law#

    Magnetic fields#

    Magnetic fields are made by moving charges, and they push only on moving charges. Two parallel wires carrying current the same way attract; carrying current opposite ways, they repel. Electrostatics can't explain this, because both wires are electrically neutral. Something new is going on: magnetism.

    The force law#

    The magnetic force on a charge \( Q \) moving with velocity \( \vec{v} \) is

    \[\vec{F}_{\text{mag}} = Q\left( \vec{v} \cross \vec{B} \right)\]

    Add the electric force to get the Lorentz force law:

    \[\vec{F} = Q\left[ \vec{E} + \left( \vec{v} \cross \vec{B} \right) \right]\]

    The magnetic field \( \vec{B} \) is measured in teslas (T). One tesla is one newton per ampere-metre. (A fridge magnet is about 0.01 T; an MRI scanner is 1.5–3 T.)

    Magnetic forces do no work#

    The cross product makes the magnetic force always perpendicular to the velocity. So

    \[\dd{W} = \vec{F}_{\text{mag}}\cdot\dd{\vec{l}} = Q(\vec{v}\cross\vec{B})\cdot\vec{v}\dd{t} = 0\]

    always. A magnetic force can change the direction a charge moves, but never its speed, so it never changes its kinetic energy. It's like a ball on a string being whirled round: the string turns the ball but doesn't speed it up.

    This is a real rule, not a technicality, and it raises an obvious question: magnets do lift paper clips, so who does the work? The answer is subtle and comes in §8.3. In short: something else supplies the energy, and the magnetic force only redirects it.

    Cyclotron motion#

    A charge moving at right angles to a uniform field feels a force of constant size, always sideways to its motion. So it moves in a circle. Set the magnetic force equal to the force needed for circular motion:

    \[QvB = \frac{mv^2}{R} \quad \Rightarrow \quad R = \frac{mv}{QB}\]

    The angular frequency, called the cyclotron frequency, is

    \[\omega = \frac{v}{R} = \frac{QB}{m}\]

    It doesn't depend on the speed. A faster particle goes round a bigger circle but takes the same time per lap. That fact is how a cyclotron (a particle accelerator) works.

    If the charge also has some velocity along \( \vec{B} \), that part is unaffected, so the circle gets stretched into a helix (a corkscrew).

    Currents#

    A line charge \( \lambda \) moving at speed \( v \) is a current \( I = \lambda v \), measured in amperes. The force on a wire carrying current is

    \[\vec{F}_{\text{mag}} = \int I\left( \dd{\vec{l}} \cross \vec{B} \right)\]

    \( I \) is the same all along the wire, so it can come outside the integral.

    Current can also spread over a surface or through a volume:

    TypeDensityRelation
    surface\( \vec{K} = \sigma\vec{v} \) (A/m)\( \dd{I} = K \dd{l_\perp} \)
    volume\( \vec{J} = \rho\vec{v} \) (A/m²)\( I = \int \vec{J}\cdot\dd{\vec{a}} \)

    \( \vec{K} \) is current per unit width; \( \vec{J} \) is current per unit cross-sectional area.

    The continuity equation#

    Charge can't be created or destroyed. So if current flows out of a region, the charge inside must go down:

    \[\div{\vec{J}} = -\pdv{\rho}{t}\]

    This is local conservation of charge: charge can't disappear here and reappear somewhere else. It has to flow through the space in between.

    Magnetostatics is about steady currents: charges moving, but nothing changing with time (like water flowing steadily through a pipe). Then \( \pdv{\rho}{t} = 0 \) and

    \[\div{\vec{J}} = 0\]

    This condition holds throughout Chapters 5 and 6.

    Example#

    A charge \( Q \) moves in a region with uniform \( \vec{E} = E\vu{z} \) and \( \vec{B} = B\vu{x} \), perpendicular to one another. Determine the velocity for which the particle travels in a straight line at constant speed.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    The particle goes straight if the total force is zero, meaning the electric and magnetic forces cancel:

    \[Q\left[ \vec{E} + (\vec{v}\cross\vec{B}) \right] = 0 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \vec{v}\cross\vec{B} = -\vec{E}\]

    \( \vec{B} \) is along \( \vu{x} \) and \( \vec{E} \) along \( \vu{z} \), so we need \( \vec{v}\cross\vec{B} \) along \( -\vu{z} \). Try \( \vec{v} = v\vu{y} \):

    \[\vec{v}\cross\vec{B} = v\vu{y}\cross B\vu{x} = -vB\,\vu{z}\]

    That works if \( vB = E \), so

    \[v = \frac{E}{B}\]

    along \( \vu{y} \), at right angles to both fields.

    This setup is a velocity selector. Only particles with exactly this speed go straight through, whatever their charge or mass; all others are bent aside. The speed it picks depends only on the two field strengths.

    Example#

    A current \( I \) flows along a wire bent into a semicircle of radius \( R \) lying in the \( xy \) plane, in a uniform field \( \vec{B} = B\vu{z} \). Find the net force on the semicircle.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    The force is

    \[\vec{F} = I\int \left( \dd{\vec{l}} \cross \vec{B} \right) = I\left( \int \dd{\vec{l}} \right) \cross \vec{B}\]

    \( \vec{B} \) came outside the integral because it is uniform. Now the useful observation: \( \int \dd{\vec{l}} \) is just all the little steps along the wire added up, which is the straight arrow from one end of the wire to the other.

    For a semicircle from \( (-R,0) \) to \( (R,0) \), that arrow is \( 2R\vu{x} \), however curved the path. So

    \[\vec{F} = I\left( 2R\vu{x} \right)\cross\left( B\vu{z} \right) = 2IRB\left( \vu{x}\cross\vu{z} \right) = -2IRB\,\vu{y}\]

    The semicircle feels the same force as a straight wire of length \( 2R \) joining its ends.

    It follows straight away that a closed loop in a uniform field has \( \oint \dd{\vec{l}} = 0 \) (you end where you started), so the net force is zero. A current loop in a uniform field gets twisted (a torque) but not pushed. That idea leads to magnetic dipoles in §6.1.

    Full derivation: §5.1.
