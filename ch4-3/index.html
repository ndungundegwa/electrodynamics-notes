    4.3 The Electric Displacement#

    Gauss's law in a dielectric#

    In a dielectric there are two kinds of charge. Bound charge comes from the polarization. Free charge is everything else: charge you put there on purpose and control, such as the charge on a capacitor plate.

    \[\rho = \rho_b + \rho_f\]

    Gauss's law counts all of it. Put in \( \rho_b = -\div{\vec{P}} \):

    \[\epsilon_0 \div{\vec{E}} = \rho_f - \div{\vec{P}} \quad \Rightarrow \quad \div{\left( \epsilon_0\vec{E} + \vec{P} \right)} = \rho_f\]

    Now define the electric displacement

    \[\vec{D} \equiv \epsilon_0 \vec{E} + \vec{P}\]

    and Gauss's law becomes

    \[\div{\vec{D}} = \rho_f, \qquad \oint \vec{D}\cdot\dd{\vec{a}} = Q_{f,\text{enc}}\]

    Why bother? You know the free charge, because you put it there. You don't know the bound charge: it depends on the field, which is what you're trying to find. Using \( \vec{D} \) lets you ignore the bound charge and work only with what you know.

    A trap: D is not just like E#

    It is tempting to think of \( \vec{D} \) as “\( \epsilon_0\vec{E} \) with the bound charge hidden”. That is only half true. Take the curl:

    \[\curl{\vec{D}} = \epsilon_0(\curl{\vec{E}}) + \curl{\vec{P}} = \curl{\vec{P}}\]

    This is not zero in general. So:

    There is no potential for \( \vec{D} \): it isn't the gradient of anything. Knowing the free charge is not enough to find \( \vec{D} \) in general. The symmetry trick used for \( \vec{E} \) in §2.2 only works for \( \vec{D} \) when \( \curl{\vec{P}} = 0 \) as well.

    Boundary conditions#

    The pillbox and loop arguments, applied to \( \vec{D} \), give

    \[D_{\text{above}}^{\perp} - D_{\text{below}}^{\perp} = \sigma_f, \qquad \vec{D}_{\text{above}}^{\parallel} - \vec{D}_{\text{below}}^{\parallel} = \vec{P}_{\text{above}}^{\parallel} - \vec{P}_{\text{below}}^{\parallel}\]

    The perpendicular part jumps by the free surface charge. The parallel part jumps by the change in polarization. Compare \( \vec{E} \), whose parallel part never jumps.

    A subtlety in crystals#

    “Dipole moment per unit volume” is clear for a finite object. For an endless repeating crystal it isn't. The answer depends on how you choose the repeating unit (the unit cell). Different, equally valid choices give different dipole moments per cell, and so different values of \( \vec{P} \).

    What is well defined is the change in polarization when you squeeze the crystal or apply a field, and that change is what experiments measure. The absolute value of \( \vec{P} \) in a bulk crystal is a convention; changes in it are physical.

    Example#

    A long straight wire carrying uniform line charge \( \lambda \) is surrounded by rubber insulation out to radius \( a \). Find the electric displacement.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Everything has cylindrical symmetry, and the rubber's polarization points radially, so \( \curl{\vec{P}} = 0 \). That means the symmetry trick for \( \vec{D} \) is allowed.

    Use a cylindrical Gaussian surface of radius \( s \) and length \( L \) around the wire. The free charge inside is the wire's, \( \lambda L \). The rubber's bound charge isn't free, so it doesn't count:

    \[\oint \vec{D}\cdot\dd{\vec{a}} = D(s)\,2\pi s L = \lambda L\]

    so

    \[\vec{D} = \frac{\lambda}{2\pi s}\vu{s}\]

    This is true both inside and outside the rubber, since the free charge enclosed is the same.

    Notice what just happened: we found \( \vec{D} \) without knowing anything about how the rubber polarizes. To get \( \vec{E} \) you do need that. Outside the rubber \( \vec{P} = 0 \), so \( \vec{E} = \vec{D}/\epsilon_0 \). Inside the rubber you need the extra relation given in §4.4.

    Example#

    A uniformly polarized sphere carries \( \vec{P} = P\vu{z} \) and no free charge. Find \( \vec{D} \) inside and outside, and comment on whether Gauss's law alone could have produced the answer.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    From §4.2, the field inside is \( \vec{E}_{\text{in}} = -\vec{P}/3\epsilon_0 \). So inside,

    \[\vec{D} = \epsilon_0\vec{E} + \vec{P} = \epsilon_0\left( -\frac{\vec{P}}{3\epsilon_0} \right) + \vec{P} = \frac{2}{3}\vec{P}\]

    Outside, \( \vec{P} = 0 \) and the field is a dipole field, so \( \vec{D} = \epsilon_0\vec{E}_{\text{dip}} \), which is not zero.

    Could Gauss's law alone have told us this? No. There is no free charge anywhere, so \( \div{\vec{D}} = 0 \) everywhere and the flux of \( \vec{D} \) through every closed surface is zero. Yet \( \vec{D} \) itself is not zero.

    The reason: at the sphere's surface the polarization suddenly drops to zero, so \( \curl{\vec{D}} = \curl{\vec{P}} \neq 0 \) there. A field with zero divergence but non-zero curl is not fixed by Gauss's law. Concluding “no free charge, so \( \vec{D} = 0 \)” is a common and tempting mistake. This example is exactly the warning above in action.
