    6.4 Linear and Nonlinear Media#

    Magnetic susceptibility and permeability#

    For paramagnets and diamagnets, as long as the field isn't too strong, the magnetization is proportional to the field. By convention it's written using \( \vec{H} \), not \( \vec{B} \):

    \[\vec{M} = \chi_m \vec{H}\]

    \( \chi_m \) is the magnetic susceptibility, a pure number. Materials like this are called linear media.

    Using \( \vec{H} \) here is a bit odd. The electric version, \( \vec{P} = \epsilon_0\chi_e\vec{E} \), uses \( \vec{E} \), not \( \vec{D} \). But the convention is too well established to change, and it makes the link between \( \vec{B} \) and \( \vec{H} \) simple:

    \[\vec{B} = \mu_0\left( \vec{H} + \vec{M} \right) = \mu_0(1+\chi_m)\vec{H} \equiv \mu\vec{H}\]

    \( \mu = \mu_0(1+\chi_m) \) is the permeability, and \( \mu_r = \mu/\mu_0 \) the relative permeability.

    The sign of \( \chi_m \) tells you the type:

    Type\( \chi_m \)Typical sizeBehaviour
    Paramagneticpositive\( \sim 10^{-5} \)weakly attracted
    Diamagneticnegative\( \sim 10^{-5} \)weakly repelled

    Both are tiny. That's why most everyday materials seem non-magnetic. In a uniform linear material, the bound volume current is tied to the free current:

    \[\vec{J}_b = \chi_m \vec{J}_f\]

    So if no free current flows through the material, the only bound current is on its surface.

    Ferromagnetism#

    Ferromagnets are not linear, and \( \chi_m \) doesn't make sense for them. Their magnetization doesn't just depend on the field now; it depends on what has happened to the sample before. It has a memory.

    The cause is a quantum-mechanical effect that makes neighbouring atomic dipoles line up with each other, whether or not there is an outside field. The whole piece doesn't line up in one direction, because that would cost too much energy. Instead it splits into small regions called domains. Each domain is strongly magnetized, but different domains point in random directions, so an ordinary lump of iron looks unmagnetized overall.

    Apply a field, and domains pointing along it grow while the others shrink. This is largely irreversible: remove the field and much of the alignment stays, leaving a permanent magnet. If you plot \( \vec{M} \) against \( \vec{H} \) while sweeping the field up and down, you don't get a single curve but a loop, called a hysteresis loop. The area inside the loop is energy lost as heat each cycle (that's why transformer cores get warm).

    Heat destroys ferromagnetism. Above the Curie temperature (770 °C for iron), thermal jiggling beats the aligning effect, the domains break up, and the material becomes an ordinary paramagnet. The change is sudden: a true phase transition, like ice melting.

    Example#

    An infinite solenoid with \( n \) turns per unit length carrying current \( I \) is filled with a linear magnetic material of susceptibility \( \chi_m \). Find \( \vec{H} \), \( \vec{B} \), and the bound surface current.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Use Ampère's law for \( \vec{H} \) with the usual rectangular loop. Only the free current in the windings counts:

    \[\vec{H} = nI\,\vu{z}\]

    That's the same as for an empty solenoid, since \( \vec{H} \) only cares about free current. Then

    \[\vec{B} = \mu\vec{H} = \mu_0(1+\chi_m)nI\,\vu{z}\]

    so the field is \( \mu_r \) times what it would be with the solenoid empty. The magnetization is

    \[\vec{M} = \chi_m\vec{H} = \chi_m n I \,\vu{z}\]

    It's uniform, so there's no bound volume current. The bound surface current is

    \[\vec{K}_b = \vec{M}\cross\vu{n} = \chi_m n I\,\vu{\phi}\]

    For a paramagnet (\( \chi_m > 0 \)) it flows the same way as the current in the windings, adding to it and strengthening \( \vec{B} \). For a diamagnet it flows the opposite way and weakens \( \vec{B} \). That's how the material changes the field.

    An iron core can boost the field by a factor of thousands. But iron isn't linear, so this calculation doesn't apply to it directly.

    Example#

    A current \( I \) flows down a long straight wire of radius \( a \), which is made of a linear material with susceptibility \( \chi_m \). Find the field inside the wire and the bound currents.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Take the free current to be spread evenly across the wire. Ampère's law for \( \vec{H} \) on a circle of radius \( s < a \) gives, as in §6.3,

    \[\vec{H} = \frac{Is}{2\pi a^2}\vu{\phi}\]

    Then

    \[\vec{B} = \mu\vec{H} = \frac{\mu I s}{2\pi a^2}\vu{\phi}, \qquad \vec{M} = \chi_m\vec{H} = \frac{\chi_m I s}{2\pi a^2}\vu{\phi}\]

    The magnetization is uneven, so there is a bound volume current:

    \[\vec{J}_b = \curl{\vec{M}} = \frac{1}{s}\pdv{}{s}\left( s M_\phi \right)\vu{z} = \frac{\chi_m I}{\pi a^2}\vu{z}\]

    That is \( \chi_m \) times the free current density \( J_f = I/\pi a^2 \), as the general rule says.

    The bound surface current is

    \[\vec{K}_b = \vec{M}\cross\vu{n} = \frac{\chi_m I}{2\pi a}\left( \vu{\phi}\cross\vu{s} \right) = -\frac{\chi_m I}{2\pi a}\vu{z}\]

    The total bound current is \( \chi_m I - \chi_m I = 0 \), as it must be. The volume current carries \( \chi_m I \) down the wire and the surface current brings the same amount back.

    Full derivation: §6.4.
