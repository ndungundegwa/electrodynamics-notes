    11.2 Power Radiated by a Point Charge#

    The Larmor formula#

    The fields of a moving point charge (§10.3) have two parts: a velocity field falling as \( 1/\gr^2 \), and an acceleration field falling as \( 1/\gr \). Only the second radiates:

    \[\vec{E}_{\text{rad}} = \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{\gr}{\left( \vec{\gr}\cdot\vec{u} \right)^3}\left[ \vec{\gr}\cross\left( \vec{u}\cross\vec{a} \right) \right]\]

    (The velocity field does hold energy, but that energy travels along with the particle rather than escaping to infinity.)

    For a charge that is momentarily at rest, \( \vec{u} = c\vu{\gr} \), and this simplifies to

    \[\vec{E}_{\text{rad}} = \frac{\mu_0 q}{4\pi\gr}\left[ \left( \vu{\gr}\cdot\vec{a} \right)\vu{\gr} - \vec{a} \right]\]

    with Poynting vector

    \[\vec{S}_{\text{rad}} = \frac{\mu_0 q^2 a^2}{16\pi^2 c}\left( \frac{\sin^2\theta}{\gr^2} \right)\vu{\gr}\]

    where \( \theta \) is measured from the direction of the acceleration. Again the doughnut shape: nothing goes out straight along the acceleration (forwards or backwards), and most goes out sideways.

    Add it up over a sphere. The \( \gr^2 \) in the area cancels the \( 1/\gr^2 \) exactly (that's why a \( 1/\gr \) field counts as radiation), giving the Larmor formula:

    \[P = \frac{\mu_0 q^2 a^2}{6\pi c}\]

    Radiated power goes as acceleration squared. A charge at rest doesn't radiate. A charge moving at steady velocity doesn't radiate. An accelerating charge does. The formula is accurate whenever the speed is much less than \( c \).

    The Larmor formula is for point charges. A uniformly charged ring spinning steadily has every piece accelerating, yet radiates nothing. For spread-out charges the radiation from different parts can cancel, so you can't just add up the powers.

    The Liénard formula#

    When the charge is moving fast there's a new effect: the rate at which energy arrives at a distant sphere is not the same as the rate at which it left the charge. A charge moving towards you squeezes its emission into a shorter time, just like the Doppler effect. The squeeze factor is

    \[\frac{\vec{\gr}\cdot\vec{u}}{\gr c} = 1 - \frac{\vu{\gr}\cdot\vec{v}}{c}\]

    Taking this into account and adding up over all directions gives the Liénard formula:

    \[P = \frac{\mu_0 q^2 \gamma^6}{6\pi c}\left( a^2 - \left| \frac{\vec{v}\cross\vec{a}}{c} \right|^2 \right), \qquad \gamma \equiv \frac{1}{\sqrt{1-v^2/c^2}}\]

    At low speed this becomes Larmor's formula. The \( \gamma^6 \) means the radiated power shoots up as the particle nears the speed of light. That's the main headache in building circular particle accelerators.

    Two special cases have names:

    SituationPowerName
    \( \vec{v}\parallel\vec{a} \) (speeding up or slowing down in a line)\( \mu_0 q^2 a^2\gamma^6/6\pi c \)bremsstrahlung (“braking radiation”)
    \( \vec{v}\perp\vec{a} \) (moving in a circle)\( \mu_0 q^2 a^2\gamma^4/6\pi c \)synchrotron radiation

    At speeds near \( c \), in both cases the radiation is thrown forward into a narrow cone around the direction of motion, like a searchlight. (Hospital X-ray tubes use bremsstrahlung; synchrotron light sources use the second.)

    Example#

    In the Bohr model of hydrogen, an electron orbits at radius \( 5\times10^{-11}\ \text{m} \). Estimate the power radiated classically and the resulting lifetime of the atom.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    The electron's circular acceleration comes from the Coulomb pull of the proton:

    \[a = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{e^2}{m_e r^2} = \frac{(9\times10^9)(1.6\times10^{-19})^2}{(9.11\times10^{-31})(5\times10^{-11})^2}\]

    The top is \( 2.30\times10^{-28} \) and the bottom \( 2.28\times10^{-51} \), so \( a \approx 1.0\times10^{23}\ \text{m/s}^2 \).

    By the Larmor formula,

    \[P = \frac{\mu_0 e^2 a^2}{6\pi c} = \frac{(1.26\times10^{-6})(1.6\times10^{-19})^2(1.0\times10^{23})^2}{6\pi(3\times10^8)} \approx 5.7\times10^{-8}\ \text{W}\]

    The orbit's total energy is \( E = -e^2/8\pi\epsilon_0 r \approx -2.2\times10^{-18}\ \text{J} \), so a rough lifetime is

    \[t \sim \frac{2.2\times10^{-18}}{5.7\times10^{-8}} \approx 4\times10^{-11}\ \text{s}\]

    So classical physics says every atom should collapse in about 40 picoseconds, with the electron spiralling into the nucleus. Atoms obviously don't do this. This failure is one of the clearest reasons we need quantum mechanics. Note that we haven't misused the theory: the electron's speed is much less than \( c \), so Larmor's formula should apply. The classical prediction is simply wrong.

    Example#

    An electron is accelerated in a linear accelerator and, separately, held in a circular orbit at the same speed and acceleration magnitude. Compare the radiated power in the two cases at \( \gamma = 100 \).

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    In a straight line, \( \vec{v} \) and \( \vec{a} \) point along the same line, so \( \vec{v}\cross\vec{a} = \vec{0} \) and the Liénard formula gives

    \[P_{\text{lin}} = \frac{\mu_0 q^2 a^2\gamma^6}{6\pi c}\]

    In a circle they're at right angles, so \( |\vec{v}\cross\vec{a}| = va \) and

    \[P_{\text{circ}} = \frac{\mu_0 q^2\gamma^6}{6\pi c}\left( a^2 - \frac{v^2a^2}{c^2} \right) = \frac{\mu_0 q^2 a^2 \gamma^6}{6\pi c}\left( 1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2} \right) = \frac{\mu_0 q^2 a^2\gamma^4}{6\pi c}\]

    using \( 1 - v^2/c^2 = 1/\gamma^2 \). So

    \[\frac{P_{\text{lin}}}{P_{\text{circ}}} = \gamma^2 = 10^4\]

    For the same acceleration, the straight-line case radiates 10,000 times more.

    But that's misleading in practice, because real accelerations are nothing like equal. Steering a near-light-speed beam round a ring needs a huge sideways acceleration (\( a = v^2/R \)), while a linear accelerator speeds particles up quite gently. So in real machines it's the circular ones that lose enormous energy to radiation. That's why future high-energy electron colliders are planned as straight (linear) machines, and why the highest-energy ring, the LHC, uses protons: at the same energy a proton, about 2000 times heavier, has a far smaller \( \gamma \), and since the power goes as \( \gamma^4 \), it radiates vastly less.
