    10.3 Point Charges#

    The Liénard-Wiechert potentials#

    For a single point charge \( q \) moving any way at all, the retarded potentials work out to

    \[V(\vec{r},t) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{qc}{\left( \gr c - \vec{\gr}\cdot\vec{v} \right)}, \qquad \vec{A}(\vec{r},t) = \frac{\vec{v}}{c^2}V(\vec{r},t)\]

    Everything on the right is taken at the retarded time: the moment the charge sent out the signal that is arriving now. That time is found from

    \[\left| \vec{r} - \vec{w}(t_r) \right| = c\left( t - t_r \right)\]

    where \( \vec{w}(t) \) is the charge's path. In words: the distance from where the charge was to where you are equals how far light travels in the time since. A point charge (moving slower than light) always has exactly one such moment.

    Compared with the static \( q/4\pi\epsilon_0\gr \), the new feature is the factor \( \left( \gr c - \vec{\gr}\cdot\vec{v} \right)^{-1} \). It is not a relativity effect. It comes from timing: a charge coming towards you “stays in view” of the incoming signal for slightly longer than a still one would, so it counts for more. (It's the same effect as a Doppler shift.) So a charge heading towards you has a bigger potential than a still charge at the same retarded position.

    The fields of a moving point charge#

    Differentiating the potentials (remembering that \( t_r \) depends on position and time too) gives

    \[\vec{E}(\vec{r},t) = \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{\gr}{\left( \vec{\gr}\cdot\vec{u} \right)^3}\left[ \left( c^2 - v^2 \right)\vec{u} + \vec{\gr}\cross\left( \vec{u}\cross\vec{a} \right) \right]\]

    with

    \[\vec{u} \equiv c\vu{\gr} - \vec{v}\]

    and

    \[\vec{B}(\vec{r},t) = \frac{1}{c}\vu{\gr}\cross\vec{E}(\vec{r},t)\]

    So \( \vec{B} \) is always at right angles to \( \vec{E} \) and to the line from the retarded position.

    The bracket has two parts:

    • The velocity field, with \( (c^2 - v^2)\vec{u} \). It doesn't depend on acceleration and falls as \( 1/\gr^2 \). This is Coulomb's law upgraded for a moving charge. It stays attached to the charge.
    • The acceleration field, the triple cross product. It is proportional to the acceleration \( \vec{a} \) and falls only as \( 1/\gr \). This is the radiation field: it breaks away from the charge and travels off to infinity.

    Only the acceleration field radiates, for the reason in §10.2: a distant sphere's area grows as \( \gr^2 \), so only \( 1/\gr \) fields carry energy that far. No acceleration, no radiation. Chapter 11 follows this up.

    For a charge moving at constant velocity there's no acceleration field. The field then points away from where the charge is now (not where it was), and is squashed towards the plane perpendicular to the motion.

    Example#

    Find the potentials and fields of a point charge moving with constant velocity \( \vec{v} \), and identify where the field appears to originate.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Let the charge pass through the origin at \( t=0 \), so \( \vec{w}(t) = \vec{v}t \). Solving for the retarded time and putting it into the Liénard-Wiechert potential gives

    \[V(\vec{r},t) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{qc}{\sqrt{\left( c^2 t - \vec{r}\cdot\vec{v} \right)^2 + \left( c^2 - v^2 \right)\left( r^2 - c^2t^2 \right)}}\]

    With \( \vec{a} = \vec{0} \), only the velocity field is left:

    \[\vec{E} = \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{1 - v^2/c^2}{\left( 1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}\sin^2\theta \right)^{3/2}}\frac{\vu{R}}{R^2}\]

    Here \( \vec{R} \) is the vector from where the charge is now, and \( \theta \) is the angle between \( \vec{R} \) and \( \vec{v} \).

    This is a surprising result. The potentials were built from where the charge was when the signal left. Yet the field points straight away from where the charge is now. It's as if the field “predicted” the charge's motion. The prediction works only because the velocity is constant. If the charge accelerates, the prediction goes wrong, and the error is exactly the radiation field.

    The field isn't the same in all directions. The factor \( (1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}\sin^2\theta)^{-3/2} \) makes it weaker in front of and behind the charge (\( \theta = 0, \pi \)) and stronger to the sides (\( \theta = \pi/2 \)). Near the speed of light the field is squashed into a flat pancake at right angles to the motion. Chapter 12 shows this is exactly what relativity predicts.

    Example#

    A charge moves along the \( x \)-axis with \( w(t) = \sqrt{b^2 + c^2t^2} \), so-called hyperbolic motion. Find the retarded time for a field point on the \( x \)-axis with \( 0 < x < b \), and comment on the range of validity.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    The charge comes in from \( x = +\infty \) long ago, slows down, gets closest (\( x = b \)) at \( t = 0 \), and goes back out. Its speed gets closer and closer to \( c \) far in the past and future, but never reaches it.

    The field point is at \( x < b \), so the charge is always to its right. The retarded-time condition is

    \[w(t_r) - x = c(t - t_r) \quad \Rightarrow \quad \sqrt{b^2 + c^2 t_r^2} = x + c(t - t_r)\]

    Square both sides:

    \[b^2 + c^2t_r^2 = \left( x + ct \right)^2 - 2c t_r\left( x + ct \right) + c^2t_r^2\]

    The \( c^2t_r^2 \) terms cancel, leaving a simple linear equation:

    \[t_r = \frac{(x+ct)^2 - b^2}{2c(x+ct)}\]

    This only works if \( x + ct > 0 \), i.e. \( t > -x/c \). Before that time, no signal from the charge has ever reached the field point, so the fields there are zero.

    This is real, not a maths glitch. Far in the past the charge was moving almost at the speed of light, travelling along with the light ray \( x = -ct \) and never quite catching up with it. Any signal it sends starts behind that ray, and nothing travels faster than light, so no signal can ever reach the region ahead of the ray. Examples like this are used to test the tricky ideas about radiation and radiation reaction in §11.3.
