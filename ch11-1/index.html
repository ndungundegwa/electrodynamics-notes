    11.1 Dipole Radiation#

    What radiation is#

    Radiation is energy that escapes to infinity and never comes back. The power passing through a sphere of radius \( r \) is \( \oint\vec{S}\cdot\dd{\vec{a}} \). The sphere's area grows as \( r^2 \), so for the power not to shrink to zero at huge \( r \), the Poynting vector must fall no faster than \( 1/r^2 \). Since \( \vec{S} \) is (field)², the fields must fall as \( 1/r \).

    Static fields, and the fields of steadily moving charges, fall as \( 1/r^2 \) or faster, so they carry no energy to infinity. Only terms involving how fast the sources change (equivalently, accelerating charges) give \( 1/r \) fields. So: radiation needs accelerating charge.

    Electric dipole radiation#

    Take two small metal balls a distance \( d \) apart, joined by a wire, with charge sloshing back and forth between them:

    \[q(t) = q_0\cos(\omega t), \qquad p(t) = p_0\cos(\omega t), \quad p_0 = q_0 d\]

    This is a simple model of a radio antenna. Far away (much further than both \( d \) and the wavelength \( c/\omega \)), the retarded potentials give

    \[\vec{E} = -\frac{\mu_0 p_0\omega^2}{4\pi}\left( \frac{\sin\theta}{r} \right)\cos\left[ \omega(t - r/c) \right]\vu{\theta}\] \[\vec{B} = -\frac{\mu_0 p_0\omega^2}{4\pi c}\left( \frac{\sin\theta}{r} \right)\cos\left[ \omega(t - r/c) \right]\vu{\phi}\]

    They are in step, at right angles to each other and to the direction outwards, with \( E/B = c \). Far from the source, a small patch of this wave looks just like a plane wave.

    The intensity is

    \[\langle\vec{S}\rangle = \frac{\mu_0 p_0^2\omega^4}{32\pi^2 c}\left( \frac{\sin^2\theta}{r^2} \right)\vu{r}\]

    and adding it up over a sphere gives the total power radiated:

    \[\langle P \rangle = \frac{\mu_0 p_0^2\omega^4}{12\pi c}\]

    Two things to notice:

    1. The \( \sin^2\theta \) pattern is shaped like a doughnut around the dipole's axis. Nothing is sent out along the axis itself. (That's why a vertical radio mast sends signals out sideways, not up.)

    2. The power goes as \( \omega^4 \). Double the frequency and you get 16 times the power.

    That \( \omega^4 \) is why the sky is blue. Sunlight makes air molecules oscillate like tiny dipoles, which re-radiate (scatter) the light. Violet-blue light has about 1.7 times the frequency of deep red light, so it is scattered roughly \( 1.7^4 \approx 9 \) times more strongly. Look at any part of the sky away from the Sun and you mostly see this scattered blue light. (At sunset, the sunlight reaching you has travelled through so much air that most of its blue has been scattered away, leaving red.)

    Magnetic dipole radiation#

    A loop of area \( a \) with an oscillating current \( I(t) = I_0\cos(\omega t) \) has magnetic moment \( m_0 = I_0 a \) and radiates

    \[\langle P \rangle = \frac{\mu_0 m_0^2 \omega^4}{12\pi c^3}\]

    It has the same doughnut pattern, with \( \vec{E} \) and \( \vec{B} \) swapping roles.

    For sources of similar size \( b \),

    \[\frac{P_{\text{magnetic}}}{P_{\text{electric}}} = \left( \frac{\omega b}{c} \right)^2\]

    \( \omega b/c \) is (source size)/(wavelength), which we already assumed is small, and here it's squared. So electric dipole radiation wins, unless the source has no electric dipole moment at all.

    Radiation from any source#

    For a general small source, use the multipole expansion; the first term that isn't zero dominates. The monopole term can never radiate: total charge is conserved, so \( \dot{Q} = 0 \) always. A charged ball that pulsates in and out radiates nothing, however wildly it pulsates, because by Gauss's law its outside field only depends on its total charge.

    So the electric dipole term is usually the main one. If that is zero, you go on to magnetic dipole and electric quadrupole radiation, and so on, each much weaker than the one before.

    Example#

    Estimate the power radiated by an antenna of length \( d = 1\ \text{m} \) carrying charge amplitude \( q_0 = 1\ \text{nC} \) oscillating at 100 MHz.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    The dipole moment amplitude is

    \[p_0 = q_0 d = (10^{-9})(1) = 10^{-9}\ \text{C}\cdot\text{m}\]

    and the angular frequency is

    \[\omega = 2\pi\nu = 2\pi(10^8) = 6.28\times10^8\ \text{s}^{-1}\]

    Put these in:

    \[\langle P \rangle = \frac{\mu_0 p_0^2 \omega^4}{12\pi c} = \frac{(4\pi\times10^{-7})(10^{-9})^2(6.28\times10^8)^4}{12\pi(3.00\times10^8)}\]

    The top is \( (1.26\times10^{-6})(10^{-18})(1.56\times10^{35}) = 1.96\times10^{11} \) and the bottom is \( 1.13\times10^{10} \), so

    \[\langle P \rangle \approx 17\ \text{W}\]

    That's a lot from a tiny charge, all thanks to the \( \omega^4 \). Drop the frequency by a factor of 10 and the power falls by 10,000. That's why radiating efficiently needs high frequencies, and why low-frequency antennas have to be huge.

    Caution: the wavelength here is \( c/\nu = 3 \) m, only three times the antenna length. The formula assumes \( d \ll \lambda \), which is only roughly true, so treat 17 W as an estimate.

    Example#

    Explain why a uniformly charged sphere whose radius oscillates emits no radiation, despite every part of its surface accelerating.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Outside the sphere, Gauss's law gives

    \[\vec{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{Q}{r^2}\vu{r}\]

    at any \( r \) bigger than the sphere's largest radius. It doesn't matter how the charge is arranged inside, as long as it stays spherically symmetric. So the outside field never changes, and an unchanging field can't radiate.

    In multipole terms: the monopole moment is \( Q \), which can't change because charge is conserved. The dipole and all higher moments are zero by symmetry. So every term that could radiate is either zero or constant.

    Doesn't every accelerating charge radiate? That rule (the Larmor formula, §11.2) is for a single point charge. Here there are many charges, and their radiation fields add up as vectors. Power goes as (field)², so the total isn't simply the sum of each piece's power. For the pulsating sphere, the radiation from different parts cancels completely, in every direction.

    The same reasoning explains why a uniformly charged ring spinning steadily doesn't radiate. It's a standard warning: don't apply the point-charge formula to spread-out charges without thinking.

    Full derivation: §11.1.
