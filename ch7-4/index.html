    7.4 The Field of a Rotating Magnet#

    The question#

    Take a magnet that is symmetric about an axis (a cylindrical bar magnet, or the Earth) and spin it steadily about that axis. Does its magnetic field stay still, or does it spin round with the magnet? Are the field lines dragged round as it turns?

    This section is about ideas only; there are no new calculations. It's here because students ask this question all the time, and the answer is subtler than it looks.

    Faraday's experiments#

    Faraday tested this using the spinning disc of §7.1, with a magnet on the disc's axis, and tried every combination:

    ConfigurationResult
    Magnet still, disc rotatingcurrent flows
    Magnet and disc rotating togethercurrent flows
    Disc still, magnet rotatingno current

    Look at the pattern: only the disc's motion matters. Whether the magnet spins or not makes no difference. So Faraday concluded that a spinning magnet's field is exactly the same as a still magnet's: a magnet spinning about its symmetry axis does not drag its field round with it.

    Not everyone agreed until 1912, when experiments by S. J. Barnett convinced people Faraday was right. In particular, the part of the Earth's magnetic field that is symmetric about its spin axis does not spin with the Earth.

    Of course, a magnet spun about a different axis (say, end over end) certainly does carry its field round, because its north and south poles keep changing direction.

    What the question actually means#

    The real trouble is that the question assumes something false. Faraday pictured field lines as actual physical things strung through space, like threads. If they were, asking whether they move would make sense. But in Maxwell's theory it's the field that is real. Field lines are just a drawing we make to picture it.

    Think about the electric version. A point charge at rest has field lines like the spokes of a wheel. A second later the picture looks identical. Now ask: which line now is the same line as a particular one a second ago? There is no answer, because the lines have no labels. Someone who insists the spokes are rotating isn't wrong (the picture still shows the right field at every moment), just unhelpful.

    Field lines tell you exactly two things: how closely packed they are gives the field's strength, and which way they point gives its direction. Once those are right, there is nothing more to know. “Which line is which” from one moment to the next is not a physics question, and it has no right answer.

    Why the magnetic case is more troublesome#

    For electric fields that settles it. The magnetic case is trickier because of the \( \vec{v} \) in the Lorentz force law. Velocity measured relative to what?

    The modern answer: relative to whatever (non-accelerating) frame you're working in, the same frame in which you measure \( \vec{B} \). But if you picture field lines as wires strung through space, it's easy to convince yourself (as 19th-century physicists did) that \( \vec{v} \) must mean velocity relative to the field lines. That way of talking survives in engineering, where people say a conductor “cuts” field lines.

    If that idea were right, Faraday would have seen a current when only the magnet spun, and no current when magnet and disc spun together (no relative motion). He saw the exact opposite.

    “Moving field lines” do not create electric fields. Fields that change with time do. These are not the same thing, and mixing them up causes the confusion. (A symmetric magnet spinning about its axis produces a field that doesn't change with time.)

    The upshot#

    Whether you say field lines are “dragged round” or not is a choice of words, not something the laws of physics decide. It can be a helpful picture of how a field changes, which is why geophysicists, plasma physicists and astrophysicists use it a lot.

    When you read such a statement, work out what the author actually means, and remember: field lines don't carry serial numbers, so you can't follow one particular line from one frame of the movie to the next.
