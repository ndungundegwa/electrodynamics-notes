    1.4 Curvilinear Coordinates#

    Choosing coordinates that match the shape of the problem saves a lot of work. A charged ball is a nightmare in \( x, y, z \) and easy in spherical coordinates. Two systems cover almost everything in this course.

    Spherical coordinates#

    \( (r, \theta, \phi) \):

    • \( r \) is the distance from the origin (\( r \geq 0 \));
    • \( \theta \) is the angle down from the \( z \)-axis, like latitude measured from the North Pole (\( 0 \leq \theta \leq \pi \));
    • \( \phi \) is the angle round from the \( x \)-axis, like longitude (\( 0 \leq \phi < 2\pi \)).
    \[x = r\sin\theta\cos\phi, \qquad y = r\sin\theta\sin\phi, \qquad z = r\cos\theta\]

    The three unit vectors are mutually perpendicular and right-handed, \( \vu{r} \cross \vu{\theta} = \vu{\phi} \). In Cartesian terms,

    \[\vu{r} = \sin\theta\cos\phi\,\vu{x} + \sin\theta\sin\phi\,\vu{y} + \cos\theta\,\vu{z}\]

    A small step, and a small volume, are

    \[\dd{\vec{l}} = \dd{r}\,\vu{r} + r\dd{\theta}\,\vu{\theta} + r\sin\theta \dd{\phi}\,\vu{\phi}\] \[\dd{\tau} = r^2 \sin\theta \dd{r}\dd{\theta}\dd{\phi}\]

    On a sphere of radius \( R \), a small patch of area is \( \dd{a} = R^2\sin\theta \dd{\theta}\dd{\phi} \). The angular part alone adds up to \( \int \sin\theta \dd{\theta}\dd{\phi} = 4\pi \) (the “total solid angle”).

    Cylindrical coordinates#

    \( (s, \phi, z) \), where \( s \) is the distance from the \( z \)-axis, \( \phi \) is the same angle as above, and \( z \) is the usual height.

    \[x = s\cos\phi, \qquad y = s\sin\phi, \qquad z = z\]\[\dd{\vec{l}} = \dd{s}\,\vu{s} + s\dd{\phi}\,\vu{\phi} + \dd{z}\,\vu{z}, \qquad \dd{\tau} = s \dd{s}\dd{\phi}\dd{z}\]

    Griffiths uses \( s \) for distance from the \( z \)-axis and keeps \( r \) for distance from the origin. Other books use \( \rho \) or \( r \) for \( s \). They are different: \( s = r\sin\theta \). Mixing them up is a common mistake when comparing formulas from different books.

    Where the extra factors come from#

    Angles are not lengths. That one fact explains every extra factor above:

    • changing \( r \) by \( \dd{r} \) moves you a distance \( \dd{r} \);
    • changing \( \theta \) by \( \dd{\theta} \) moves you along an arc of length \( r\dd{\theta} \) (a bigger distance the further you are from the origin);
    • changing \( \phi \) by \( \dd{\phi} \) moves you \( r\sin\theta \dd{\phi} \), because the circles of constant \( \theta \) shrink towards the poles (lines of longitude get closer together near the North Pole).

    Multiply the three lengths to get the volume element.

    The same factors show up inside the derivatives. In spherical coordinates,

    \[\grad t = \pdv{t}{r}\vu{r} + \frac{1}{r}\pdv{t}{\theta}\vu{\theta} + \frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\pdv{t}{\phi}\vu{\phi}\] \[\div{\vec{v}} = \frac{1}{r^2}\pdv{}{r}\left( r^2 v_r \right) + \frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\pdv{}{\theta}\left( \sin\theta\, v_\theta \right) + \frac{1}{r\sin\theta}\pdv{v_\phi}{\phi}\]

    and in cylindrical coordinates,

    \[\div{\vec{v}} = \frac{1}{s}\pdv{}{s}\left( s v_s \right) + \frac{1}{s}\pdv{v_\phi}{\phi} + \pdv{v_z}{z}\]

    You don't need to memorise these. The full list, including curl and Laplacian, is inside the front cover of Griffiths.

    Unit vectors that change direction#

    \( \vu{x}, \vu{y}, \vu{z} \) point the same way everywhere. \( \vu{r}, \vu{\theta}, \vu{\phi} \) do not: \( \vu{r} \) points “outward”, which is a different direction at every point. This has two consequences:

    1. You cannot pull them outside an integral. To integrate a vector, first write it in \( \vu{x}, \vu{y}, \vu{z} \) components.
    2. When you differentiate, the unit vectors change too. That is where the extra terms in the formulas above come from.

    Example#

    Compute the volume of a sphere of radius \( R \), and the surface area of a hemisphere of the same radius, by direct integration.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Volume. Write out the volume element in full:

    \[V = \int_0^R \!\! \int_0^\pi \!\! \int_0^{2\pi} r^2 \sin\theta \dd{\phi} \dd{\theta} \dd{r}\]

    The three integrals are independent:

    \[\int_0^{2\pi}\!\dd{\phi} = 2\pi, \qquad \int_0^\pi\!\sin\theta \dd{\theta} = 2, \qquad \int_0^R\! r^2 \dd{r} = \frac{R^3}{3}\]

    so \( V = 2\pi \cdot 2 \cdot R^3/3 = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 \). The angles together give \( 4\pi \), the total solid angle.

    Hemisphere area. On the surface, \( r = R \) is fixed, so \( \dd{a} = R^2\sin\theta \dd{\theta}\dd{\phi} \). For the top half, \( \theta \) runs from \( 0 \) to \( \pi/2 \):

    \[A = R^2 \int_0^{2\pi} \!\! \int_0^{\pi/2} \sin\theta \dd{\theta} \dd{\phi} = R^2 (2\pi)(1) = 2\pi R^2\]

    The \( \theta \) integral is now 1 instead of 2, so you get half the area of the whole sphere, as expected.

    Example#

    Verify the divergence theorem for \( \vec{v} = r^2 \vu{r} \) over a sphere of radius \( R \) centred on the origin.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Volume side. The field only has an \( r \) component, so

    \[\div{\vec{v}} = \frac{1}{r^2}\pdv{}{r}\left( r^2 \cdot r^2 \right) = \frac{1}{r^2}\left( 4r^3 \right) = 4r\]

    Integrate over the ball:

    \[\int (\div{\vec{v}}) \dd{\tau} = \int_0^R (4r)\, r^2 \dd{r} \int \sin\theta \dd{\theta}\dd{\phi} = 4\frac{R^4}{4}(4\pi) = 4\pi R^4\]

    Surface side. On the sphere, \( \dd{\vec{a}} = R^2 \sin\theta \dd{\theta}\dd{\phi},\vu{r} \) and \( \vec{v} = R^2\vu{r} \), so the dot product is \( R^4 \sin\theta \):

    \[\oint \vec{v} \cdot \dd{\vec{a}} = R^4 \int \sin\theta \dd{\theta}\dd{\phi} = 4\pi R^4\]

    The two sides agree. ✓ It was quick only because the coordinates suit the shape; in \( x, y, z \) the same check takes far more work.

    Full derivation: §1.4.
