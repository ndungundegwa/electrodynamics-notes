    7.2 Electromagnetic Induction#

    Faraday's law#

    Faraday found that an emf appears in a loop whenever the flux through it changes, and it doesn't matter how the change happens:

    1. move the loop through a steady field (motional emf, explained in §7.1 by the magnetic force on the charges);
    2. keep the loop still and move the magnet;
    3. keep everything still and change the field's strength (e.g. turn up the current in an electromagnet).

    The Lorentz force explains case 1. It can't explain cases 2 and 3: the charges in the loop aren't moving, so there's no magnetic force on them. Something else is pushing the current, and the only candidate is an electric field.

    Faraday's conclusion: a changing magnetic field creates an electric field.

    \[\mathcal{E} = \oint \vec{E}\cdot\dd{\vec{l}} = -\dv{\Phi}{t} = -\int \pdv{\vec{B}}{t}\cdot\dd{\vec{a}}\]

    Using Stokes' theorem gives the differential form:

    \[\curl{\vec{E}} = -\pdv{\vec{B}}{t}\]

    This is another of Maxwell's equations. It replaces the electrostatic rule \( \curl{\vec{E}} = \vec{0} \), which turns out to be just the special case where nothing changes with time.

    This matters: the induced electric field swirls. It is not conservative. It can't be written as the gradient of a potential, and its line integral round a closed loop is not zero. (That's the whole point: it pushes charge round the loop.) So the potential methods of Chapters 2–3 don't work for it.

    A shortcut: copy magnetostatics#

    \( \curl{\vec{E}} = -\pdv{\vec{B}}{t} \) has exactly the same form as Ampère's law \( \curl{\vec{B}} = \mu_0\vec{J} \), with \( -\pdv{\vec{B}}{t} \) in place of \( \mu_0\vec{J} \). So every magnetostatics answer can be reused: wherever a current would make a certain \( \vec{B} \), a changing field \( -\pdv{\vec{B}}{t} \) of the same shape makes an \( \vec{E} \) of the same shape.

    For symmetric problems, this is the fastest method: use the integral form just like an Ampère's-law loop.

    Inductance#

    A current \( I_1 \) in one loop sends flux through a second loop:

    \[\Phi_2 = M_{21}I_1\]

    \( M_{21} \) is the mutual inductance, and it depends only on the shapes and positions of the loops. A surprising fact is that

    \[M_{12} = M_{21} \equiv M\]

    Current \( I \) in loop 1 sends the same flux through loop 2 as current \( I \) in loop 2 sends through loop 1, however different the loops are. This is often the easiest way to find \( M \): work it out whichever way round is simpler.

    A changing current also creates an emf in its own loop:

    \[\Phi = LI, \qquad \mathcal{E} = -L\dv{I}{t}\]

    \( L \) is the self-inductance, measured in henries (H). By Lenz's law, this “back emf” fights any change in the current. So inductance acts like inertia for current: the current in an inductor can't change suddenly, just as a heavy object can't suddenly change speed.

    Energy in magnetic fields#

    Starting a current takes work against the back emf. Adding it up,

    \[W = \frac{1}{2}LI^2\]

    (compare kinetic energy \( \frac{1}{2}mv^2 \), with \( L \) like mass). Written in terms of the field instead of the circuit,

    \[W = \frac{1}{2\mu_0}\int_{\text{all space}} B^2 \dd{\tau}\]

    which matches the electric \( W = \frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int E^2 \dd{\tau} \) of §2.4. Put them together to get the total energy per unit volume stored in an electromagnetic field:

    \[u = \frac{1}{2}\left( \epsilon_0 E^2 + \frac{1}{\mu_0}B^2 \right)\]

    You will use this throughout Chapter 8.

    Example#

    Find the self-inductance of a long solenoid of length \( l \), radius \( R \), with \( n \) turns per unit length. Verify the stored energy two ways.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    The field inside is \( B = \mu_0 n I \), uniform, and zero outside. The flux through one turn is \( B\pi R^2 \). There are \( nl \) turns, and the flux passes through every one of them:

    \[\Phi = (nl)\left( \mu_0 n I \pi R^2 \right) = \mu_0 n^2 l \pi R^2 I\]

    So

    \[L = \frac{\Phi}{I} = \mu_0 n^2 \pi R^2 l\]

    It depends only on the geometry and how tightly it's wound, as it should.

    Energy from the circuit:

    \[W = \frac{1}{2}LI^2 = \frac{1}{2}\mu_0 n^2\pi R^2 l I^2\]

    Energy from the field: the field fills the inside, volume \( \pi R^2 l \), and is uniform, so

    \[W = \frac{1}{2\mu_0}B^2\left( \pi R^2 l \right) = \frac{1}{2\mu_0}\left( \mu_0 n I \right)^2 \pi R^2 l = \frac{1}{2}\mu_0 n^2 \pi R^2 l I^2\]

    The two agree. ✓

    Example#

    A short circular loop of radius \( a \) lies on the axis of a long solenoid of radius \( b \) with \( n \) turns per unit length, where \( a \ll b \). Find the mutual inductance.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    The hard way: run current through the small loop and find the flux through the whole solenoid. The small loop's field is a messy dipole field, and you'd have to integrate it over every turn of the solenoid.

    The easy way: use \( M_{12} = M_{21} \) and go the other way round. Run current \( I \) through the solenoid. Its field inside is uniform:

    \[B = \mu_0 n I\]

    The small loop sits inside this uniform field, so the flux through it is just field × area:

    \[\Phi_{\text{loop}} = \mu_0 n I \pi a^2\]

    So

    \[M = \frac{\Phi_{\text{loop}}}{I} = \mu_0 n \pi a^2\]

    By reciprocity, that's also the flux through the solenoid per unit current in the small loop. We got it without ever touching the dipole field.

    Notice that \( M \) depends on the small loop's radius but not the solenoid's, as long as the loop is inside where the field is uniform.

    Full derivation: §7.2.
