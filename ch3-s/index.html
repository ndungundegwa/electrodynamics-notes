    3.S Green's Functions#

    Supplement: beyond Griffiths, but built on him. Read it after §3.1–3.2. It uses Griffiths' SI units and notation throughout (\( \gr \), \( \mathscr{V} \), \( \mathscr{S} \), \( \dd{\tau} \), \( \dd{\vec{a}} \)), and every step points back to the equation in Griffiths it comes from (5th edition numbering). The only new idea is the Green's function \( G \) itself. For further reading: Jackson, Classical Electrodynamics, §1.8–1.10, and Zangwill, Modern Electrodynamics, §8.4–8.5. Jackson uses Gaussian units, so his \( G \) is \( 4\pi\epsilon_0 \) times ours.

    The idea#

    You have already met a Green's function without the name. Griffiths Eq. 2.29,

    \[V(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int \frac{\rho(\vec{r'})}{\gr}\dd{\tau'},\]

    builds the potential of any charge out of the potential of a unit point charge, \( 1/4\pi\epsilon_0\gr \). That is the Green's function for empty space. But Eq. 2.29 only works when there are no conductors. With conductors, the induced charge is unknown (the whole problem of Chapter 3).

    The uniqueness theorem (Griffiths §3.1.5) says the potential in a region \( \mathscr{V} \) is fixed once you know the charge inside and the potential on the boundary \( \mathscr{S} \). A Green's function turns that into a recipe:

    1. Solve the problem once, for a single unit point charge, with the boundary grounded. That answer is the Green's function \( G \).
    2. Every other problem with the same boundary is then just a sum (integral) of these unit answers, exactly as in Eq. 2.29.

    For planes and spheres, the method of images (Griffiths §3.2) gives you step 1 for free: \( G \) is Eq. 3.9 or Eq. 3.17 with \( q = 1 \).

    One dimension#

    For grounded plates at \( x = 0 \) and \( x = L \), the potential of a sheet of unit surface charge at \( x' \) satisfies Poisson's equation (Eq. 2.24) in one dimension, \( d^2G/dx^2 = -\delta(x - x')/\epsilon_0 \), and vanishes at both plates. With no charge on either side of the sheet, it is a straight line on each side (Eq. 3.6). It is continuous at the sheet, and its slope jumps by \( -1/\epsilon_0 \) there (Eq. 2.36 with \( \sigma = 1 \)):

    \[G(x, x') = \frac{x_<\,(L - x_>)}{\epsilon_0 L}\]

    where \( x_< \) and \( x_> \) denote the smaller and larger of \( x \) and \( x' \). The graph is a tent that peaks at the sheet. Swapping \( x \) and \( x' \) doesn't change it. Any charge density is just many thin sheets added together, so

    \[V(x) = \int_0^L \rho(x')\,G(x, x')\dd{x'}\]

    This is the set-up of Griffiths Prob. 3.55(a): a charge between two grounded plates.

    Green's second identity#

    This is Griffiths Prob. 1.61. Put \( \vec{v} = T\grad U \) into the divergence theorem (Eq. 1.56) and use product rule (iii), \( \div{(T\grad U)} = T\laplacian U + \grad T\cdot\grad U \). That gives Green's identity (Prob. 1.61c). Write it again with \( T \) and \( U \) swapped and subtract, and you get Green's second identity (Prob. 1.61d):

    \[\int_{\mathscr{V}} \left( T \laplacian U - U \laplacian T \right) \dd{\tau} = \oint_{\mathscr{S}} \left( T \grad U - U \grad T \right) \cdot \dd{\vec{a}}\]

    On the surface, \( \dd{\vec{a}} = \vu{n}\dd{a} \) with \( \vu{n} \) the outward normal, and \( \grad U\cdot\vu{n} = \partial U/\partial n \) is the normal derivative of Eq. 2.36. So the right-hand side can also be written

    \[\oint_{\mathscr{S}} \left( T \pdv{U}{n} - U \pdv{T}{n} \right) \dd{a}\]

    which is the form we use below. Griffiths himself uses Green's identity to prove the second uniqueness theorem (Prob. 3.6). (Careful with names: Griffiths also calls the divergence theorem itself “Green's theorem”, §1.3.4. The identity is a different result.)

    The integral formula#

    Define \( G \) by Poisson's equation for a unit point charge at \( \vec{r} \):

    \[\nabla'^2 G(\vec{r}, \vec{r'}) = -\frac{1}{\epsilon_0}\,\delta^3(\gr)\]

    By Eq. 1.102, \( \laplacian(1/\gr) = -4\pi\delta^3(\gr) \), so the Coulomb potential \( 1/4\pi\epsilon_0\gr \) is one solution. The general one is

    \[G = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0\gr} + F\]

    where \( F \) satisfies Laplace's equation (Eq. 2.25) inside \( \mathscr{V} \). Think of \( F \) as the potential of charges outside the region, such as image charges. The prime on \( \nabla' \) means we differentiate with respect to \( \vec{r'} \), the integration variable.

    Now set \( T = V \) and \( U = G \) in Green's second identity, integrating over \( \vec{r'} \). Poisson's equation gives \( \nabla'^2 V = -\rho/\epsilon_0 \), and the delta function picks out \( V(\vec{r}) \):

    \[V(\vec{r}) = \int_{\mathscr{V}} \rho\, G \dd{\tau'} + \epsilon_0 \oint_{\mathscr{S}} \left[ G \pdv{V}{n'} - V \pdv{G}{n'} \right] \dd{a'}\]

    Check: with no boundary at all (all space), the surface term vanishes, \( F = 0 \), and this is Eq. 2.29 again.

    This is not a solution yet. It needs both \( V \) and its slope \( \partial V/\partial n \) on the boundary, but you can't choose both freely: by uniqueness (§3.1.5), fixing one fixes the other. So we use our freedom to choose \( F \) to make one of the two terms disappear.

    Dirichlet and Neumann Green's functions#

    These are the two kinds of boundary data allowed by uniqueness: \( V \) itself (the first uniqueness theorem, §3.1.5) or its normal derivative \( \partial V/\partial n \) (Griffiths Prob. 3.5).

    Dirichlet (\( V \) given on \( \mathscr{S} \)). Choose \( G_D = 0 \) on \( \mathscr{S} \):

    \[V(\vec{r}) = \int_{\mathscr{V}} \rho\, G_D \dd{\tau'} - \epsilon_0 \oint_{\mathscr{S}} V \pdv{G_D}{n'} \dd{a'}\]

    \( G_D \) is the potential of a unit charge with the boundary grounded. The factor \( \epsilon_0, \partial G_D/\partial n' \) in the surface term has a physical meaning: it is the charge that a unit charge at \( \vec{r} \) induces on the grounded boundary. Compare Griffiths Eq. 2.49, \( \sigma = -\epsilon_0,\partial V/\partial n \). The minus sign is gone because our normal points out of the region, which means into the conductor, the opposite way to Griffiths' \( \vu{n} \) in Eq. 2.49.

    Neumann (\( \partial V/\partial n \) given on \( \mathscr{S} \)). The obvious choice \( \partial G/\partial n' = 0 \) is not allowed: by Gauss's law, a unit charge must send a total flux of \( 1/\epsilon_0 \) through \( \mathscr{S} \). (Griffiths warns about the same thing in his comment on Prob. 3.5: any \( \partial V/\partial n \) you specify must agree with Gauss's law.) The simplest allowed choice is \( \partial G_N/\partial n' = -1/\epsilon_0 A \), where \( A \) is the area of \( \mathscr{S} \):

    \[V(\vec{r}) = \langle V \rangle_{\mathscr{S}} + \int_{\mathscr{V}} \rho\, G_N \dd{\tau'} + \epsilon_0 \oint_{\mathscr{S}} G_N \pdv{V}{n'} \dd{a'}\]

    The average \( \langle V \rangle_{\mathscr{S}} \) is just a constant you can't determine, which is fine, since adding a constant to \( V \) doesn't change \( \vec{E} \).

    Reciprocity. \( G_D(\vec{r_1}, \vec{r_2}) = G_D(\vec{r_2}, \vec{r_1}) \): a unit charge at A produces the same potential at B as a unit charge at B produces at A, whatever the shape of the grounded conductors. This is a special case of Green's reciprocity theorem, Griffiths Prob. 3.54 (Eq. 3.107), \( \int \rho_1 V_2 \dd{\tau} = \int \rho_2 V_1 \dd{\tau} \). Prob. 3.55 shows how useful it is.

    Plane and sphere#

    Half-space \( z > 0 \) above a plane held at \( V(x', y', 0) \), with no charge in the region. Here \( G_D \) is the image potential Eq. 3.9 with \( q = 1 \), and the Dirichlet formula gives

    \[V(x, y, z) = \frac{z}{2\pi}\iint \frac{V(x', y', 0)}{[(x - x')^2 + (y - y')^2 + z^2]^{3/2}}\dd{x'}\dd{y'}\]

    Sphere of radius \( R \) held at \( V(\theta', \phi') \), with no charge in the region. Here \( G_D \) comes from the sphere image, Eqs. 3.16–3.17. With \( \alpha \) the angle between \( \vec{r} \) and \( \vec{r'} \) (the same angle as in the multipole expansion, Eq. 3.94):

    \[V(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi}\oint V(\theta', \phi')\, \frac{R\,\lvert r^2 - R^2\rvert}{(r^2 + R^2 - 2rR\cos\alpha)^{3/2}} \dd{\Omega'}\]

    The same formula holds outside (\( r > R \)) and inside (\( r < R \)). At the centre (\( r = 0 \)) it reduces to the average value property of §3.1.4.

    Example#

    A disc of radius \( a \), set flush in an infinite grounded plane and insulated from it, is held at \( V_0 \). Find the potential on its axis.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    The potential on the plane is \( V_0 \) on the disc and zero elsewhere, so only the disc contributes. On the axis, the half-space formula in polar coordinates \( (s, \phi) \) gives

    \[V(z) = \frac{zV_0}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^a \frac{s\dd{s}\dd{\phi}}{(s^2 + z^2)^{3/2}} = V_0\left[ 1 - \frac{z}{\sqrt{z^2 + a^2}} \right]\]

    As \( z \to 0 \), \( V \to V_0 \). Far away (\( z \gg a \)), \( V \approx V_0 a^2/2z^2 \), falling off like a dipole. No arrangement of image charges gives this boundary condition, but the Green's function handles it directly.

    Example#

    Use the one-dimensional Green's function to find the potential of a uniform charge density \( \rho_0 \) between grounded plates at \( x = 0 \) and \( x = L \).

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Split the integral at \( x' = x \), since \( G \) has a different formula on each side:

    \[V(x) = \frac{\rho_0}{\epsilon_0 L}\left[ (L - x)\int_0^x x'\dd{x'} + x\int_x^L (L - x')\dd{x'} \right] = \frac{\rho_0}{\epsilon_0 L}\left[ \frac{(L - x)x^2}{2} + \frac{x(L - x)^2}{2} \right] = \frac{\rho_0}{2\epsilon_0}\,x(L - x)\]

    Check: this is zero at both plates and satisfies \( V'' = -\rho_0/\epsilon_0 \) (Eq. 2.24 in one dimension), the same answer you get by integrating Poisson's equation twice. ✓
