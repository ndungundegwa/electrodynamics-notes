    3.S Green's Functions#

    Supplement: not in Griffiths. This page is more advanced than the rest; read it after §3.1–3.2. Sources: Jackson, Classical Electrodynamics, §1.8–1.10 and §2.6; Zangwill, Modern Electrodynamics, §8.4–8.5. The Green's function here is the potential of a unit point charge, as in Zangwill; Jackson's \( G \) is \( 4\pi\epsilon_0 \) times larger.

    The idea#

    The uniqueness theorem (§3.1) says the potential in a region \( \mathscr{V} \) is fixed once you know the charge inside and the potential on the boundary \( \mathscr{S} \). A Green's function turns that into a recipe:

    1. Solve the problem once, for a single unit point charge, with the boundary grounded. That answer is the Green's function \( G \).
    2. Every other problem with the same boundary is then just a sum (integral) of these unit answers.

    For planes and spheres, the method of images (§3.2) gives you step 1 for free.

    One dimension#

    For grounded plates at \( x = 0 \) and \( x = L \), the potential of a sheet of unit surface charge at \( x' \) satisfies \( d^2G/dx^2 = -\delta(x - x')/\epsilon_0 \) and vanishes at both plates. With no charge on either side of the sheet, it is a straight line on each side. It is continuous at the sheet, and its slope jumps by \( -1/\epsilon_0 \) there:

    \[G(x, x') = \frac{x_<\,(L - x_>)}{\epsilon_0 L}\]

    where \( x_< \) and \( x_> \) denote the smaller and larger of \( x \) and \( x' \). The graph is a tent that peaks at the sheet. Swapping \( x \) and \( x' \) doesn't change it. Any charge density is just many thin sheets added together, so

    \[V(x) = \int_0^L \rho(x')\,G(x, x')\dd{x'}\]

    Green's second identity#

    From \( \div{(\phi\grad\psi)} = \phi\laplacian\psi + \grad\phi\cdot\grad\psi \) and the divergence theorem, written once with \( \phi \) and \( \psi \) swapped and subtracted:

    \[\int_{\mathscr{V}} \left( \phi \laplacian \psi - \psi \laplacian \phi \right) \dd{\tau} = \oint_{\mathscr{S}} \left( \phi \pdv{\psi}{n} - \psi \pdv{\phi}{n} \right) \dd{a}\]

    The normal derivative is taken along the outward normal. (Careful: Griffiths calls the divergence theorem itself “Green's theorem”. This identity is a different result.)

    The integral formula#

    Define \( G \) by \( \nabla'^2 G(\vec{r}, \vec{r'}) = -\delta^3(\vec{r} - \vec{r'})/\epsilon_0 \), so that \( G = 1/4\pi\epsilon_0\gr + F \), where \( F \) satisfies Laplace's equation in \( \mathscr{V} \) (think of \( F \) as the potential of charges outside the region, such as image charges). Setting \( \phi = V \) and \( \psi = G \) in the identity:

    \[V(\vec{r}) = \int_{\mathscr{V}} \rho\, G \dd{\tau'} + \epsilon_0 \oint_{\mathscr{S}} \left[ G \pdv{V}{n'} - V \pdv{G}{n'} \right] \dd{a'}\]

    This is not a solution yet. It needs both \( V \) and its slope \( \partial V/\partial n \) on the boundary, but you can't choose both freely (fixing one fixes the other). So we use our freedom to choose \( F \) to make one of the two terms disappear.

    Dirichlet and Neumann Green's functions#

    Dirichlet (\( V \) given on \( \mathscr{S} \)). Choose \( G_D = 0 \) on \( \mathscr{S} \):

    \[V(\vec{r}) = \int_{\mathscr{V}} \rho\, G_D \dd{\tau'} - \epsilon_0 \oint_{\mathscr{S}} V \pdv{G_D}{n'} \dd{a'}\]

    \( G_D \) is the potential of a unit charge with the boundary grounded. The factor \( \epsilon_0, \partial G_D/\partial n' \) in the surface term has a physical meaning: it is the charge that a unit charge at \( \vec{r} \) would induce on the grounded boundary.

    Neumann (\( \partial V/\partial n \) given on \( \mathscr{S} \)). The obvious choice \( \partial G/\partial n' = 0 \) is not allowed: by Gauss's law, a unit charge must send a total flux of \( 1/\epsilon_0 \) through \( \mathscr{S} \). The simplest allowed choice is \( \partial G_N/\partial n' = -1/\epsilon_0 A \), where \( A \) is the area of \( \mathscr{S} \):

    \[V(\vec{r}) = \langle V \rangle_{\mathscr{S}} + \int_{\mathscr{V}} \rho\, G_N \dd{\tau'} + \epsilon_0 \oint_{\mathscr{S}} G_N \pdv{V}{n'} \dd{a'}\]

    The average \( \langle V \rangle_{\mathscr{S}} \) is just a constant you can't determine, which is fine, since adding a constant to \( V \) doesn't change \( \vec{E} \).

    Reciprocity. \( G_D(\vec{r_1}, \vec{r_2}) = G_D(\vec{r_2}, \vec{r_1}) \): a unit charge at A produces the same potential at B as a unit charge at B produces at A, whatever the shape of the grounded conductors. This is not obvious at all, and it is often useful.

    Plane and sphere#

    Half-space \( z > 0 \) above a plane held at \( V(x', y', 0) \), with no charge in the region:

    \[V(x, y, z) = \frac{z}{2\pi}\iint \frac{V(x', y', 0)}{[(x - x')^2 + (y - y')^2 + z^2]^{3/2}}\dd{x'}\dd{y'}\]

    Sphere of radius \( R \) held at \( V(\theta', \phi') \), with no charge in the region and \( \gamma \) the angle between \( \vec{r} \) and \( \vec{r'} \):

    \[V(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi}\oint V(\theta', \phi')\, \frac{R\,\lvert r^2 - R^2\rvert}{(r^2 + R^2 - 2rR\cos\gamma)^{3/2}} \dd{\Omega'}\]

    The same formula holds outside (\( r > R \)) and inside (\( r < R \)). At the centre (\( r = 0 \)) it reduces to the average value property of §3.1.

    Boundary dataCondition on \( G \)
    \( V \) (Dirichlet)\( G_D = 0 \) on \( \mathscr{S} \)
    \( \partial V/\partial n \) (Neumann)\( \partial G_N/\partial n' = -1/\epsilon_0 A \)
    Both on the same surface (Cauchy)Too many conditions: usually no solution

    The normal points out of the region \( \mathscr{V} \), not out of the conductor. For a problem outside a conductor, that means it points into the conductor.

    Example#

    A disc of radius \( a \), set flush in an infinite grounded plane and insulated from it, is held at \( V_0 \). Find the potential on its axis.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    The potential on the plane is \( V_0 \) on the disc and zero elsewhere, so only the disc contributes. On the axis, the half-space formula in polar coordinates \( (s, \phi) \) gives

    \[V(z) = \frac{zV_0}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\!\int_0^a \frac{s\dd{s}\dd{\phi}}{(s^2 + z^2)^{3/2}} = V_0\left[ 1 - \frac{z}{\sqrt{z^2 + a^2}} \right]\]

    As \( z \to 0 \), \( V \to V_0 \). Far away (\( z \gg a \)), \( V \approx V_0 a^2/2z^2 \), falling off like a dipole. No arrangement of image charges gives this boundary condition, but the Green's function handles it directly.

    Example#

    Use the one-dimensional Green's function to find the potential of a uniform charge density \( \rho_0 \) between grounded plates at \( x = 0 \) and \( x = L \).

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Split the integral at \( x' = x \), since \( G \) has a different formula on each side:

    \[V(x) = \frac{\rho_0}{\epsilon_0 L}\left[ (L - x)\int_0^x x'\dd{x'} + x\int_x^L (L - x')\dd{x'} \right] = \frac{\rho_0}{\epsilon_0 L}\left[ \frac{(L - x)x^2}{2} + \frac{x(L - x)^2}{2} \right] = \frac{\rho_0}{2\epsilon_0}\,x(L - x)\]

    Check: this is zero at both plates and satisfies \( V'' = -\rho_0/\epsilon_0 \), the same answer you get by integrating Poisson's equation twice. ✓
