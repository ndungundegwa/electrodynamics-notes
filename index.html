    Electrodynamics: Summary Notes#

    Summary notes for SPH 501 Electrodynamics, following David J. Griffiths, Introduction to Electrodynamics, 5th ed. (Cambridge University Press, 2024). There is one page per section of the book. Each page gives the key definitions and results, explains the ideas in plain language, and ends with worked examples. SI units throughout.

    These notes are a summary to study alongside the textbook, not a replacement for it.

    What electrodynamics is#

    Griffiths opens the book with a short section called the Advertisement, making the case for why the subject is worth your time before any equations appear. Here it is in brief.

    Where it sits. Physics has four big areas of mechanics: Newtonian mechanics for things that are slow and large, special relativity for the fast, quantum mechanics for the small, and quantum field theory for things that are both fast and small. Classical electrodynamics belongs in the first box. But unlike most classical theories, it carries over into the other three almost unchanged. It already agreed with relativity before anyone had thought of relativity. That's no accident: puzzles in electrodynamics are what led Einstein to relativity in the first place.

    Why it matters. There are only four fundamental forces. From strongest to weakest: strong, electromagnetic, weak, gravitational. That list seems too short. Where is friction, or the push from the floor that stops you falling through it, or the chemical bonds that hold molecules together? They are all electromagnetic. Apart from gravity, almost every force in everyday life is electromagnetic. The strong and weak forces only act inside atomic nuclei, and gravity is so weak that you need something the size of a planet to notice it: two electrons repel each other electrically about \( 10^{42} \) times more strongly than they attract each other by gravity.

    Why it's the model for other theories. Of the four forces, electromagnetism is the one we understand completely. Later theories were built on its pattern, including quantum chromodynamics (the strong force) and electroweak theory.

    How it came together. Electricity and magnetism started as separate subjects: one about rubbing amber and cat's fur, the other about lodestones and compass needles. In 1820 Oersted noticed that an electric current moves a compass needle. Ampère argued that magnetism is really moving charge. In 1831 Faraday found that a moving magnet drives a current. Maxwell put the pieces together into the equations we still use, and showed along the way that light is an electromagnetic wave, so optics became part of the same subject. By 1900, three separate branches of physics had become one.

    That unification runs through these notes, and it is completed in Chapter 12, where electricity and magnetism turn out to be one field seen from different points of view.

    Notation#

    SymbolMeaning
    \( \vec{r} \)field point
    \( \vec{r'} \)source point
    \( \gr \)separation vector, \( \gr \equiv \vec{r} - \vec{r'} \)
    \( s \)distance from the \( z \)-axis (cylindrical)
    \( r \)distance from the origin (spherical)

    Griffiths uses a script \( \gr \) for the vector from a charge to the point where you measure the field. It is not the same as \( \vec{r} \). Its direction \( \vu{\gr} \) changes from one bit of charge to the next, so it can't be taken outside an integral over the charge.
