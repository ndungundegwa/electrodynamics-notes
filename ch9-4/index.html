    9.4 Absorption and Dispersion#

    Waves in conductors#

    In a conductor there are free charges, so a wave's electric field drives a current \( \vec{J} = \sigma\vec{E} \), and the current term in Ampère's law is no longer zero. (Any free charge put inside a conductor quickly spreads to the surface, dying away with time constant \( \epsilon/\sigma \). So we can take \( \rho_f = 0 \), but the current stays.)

    The wave equation picks up an extra term with a first time derivative:

    \[\laplacian\vec{E} = \mu\epsilon\pdv{^2\vec{E}}{t^2} + \mu\sigma\pdv{\vec{E}}{t}\]

    That extra term acts like friction. There are still plane-wave solutions, but the wave number becomes complex, \( \tilde{k} = k + i\kappa \):

    \[\tilde{\vec{E}} = \tilde{\vec{E}}_0 e^{-\kappa z}e^{i(kz-\omega t)}\]

    The \( e^{-\kappa z} \) factor makes the wave die away as it goes in. The wave is absorbed (its energy heats the metal), and it only gets about

    \[d \equiv \frac{1}{\kappa}\]

    into the material. This is the skin depth. For a good conductor, \( \kappa \approx \sqrt{\omega\mu\sigma/2} \), so the skin depth gets smaller at higher frequency. High-frequency currents only flow in a thin layer at the surface (“skin effect”), which is why radio-frequency parts are often just silver-plated.

    Two other changes from vacuum: \( \vec{E} \) and \( \vec{B} \) are no longer in step (\( \vec{B} \) lags behind), and \( \vec{B} \) is relatively bigger than in vacuum.

    Reflection from metals#

    For a perfect conductor (\( \sigma \to \infty \)) the skin depth is zero: nothing gets in, and all the wave reflects (upside down). That's why metals are shiny: visible light bounces off instead of being absorbed or passing through.

    Real metals aren't perfect conductors, so a little light is absorbed. That's why even polished silver isn't a perfect mirror.

    Dispersion#

    So far we treated \( \epsilon \) as a constant. In reality it depends on frequency, so the wave speed \( v = c/n(\omega) \) does too. A material in which different frequencies travel at different speeds is called dispersive. A pulse is made of many frequencies, so in such a material it spreads out as it travels.

    To model this, treat the electrons bound in atoms as little masses on springs, with some friction, pushed back and forth by the wave's electric field. Solving that gives a complex permittivity, and its real part gives the index of refraction:

    \[n(\omega) = 1 + \frac{Nq^2}{2\epsilon_0 m}\sum_j \frac{f_j\left( \omega_{0j}^2 - \omega^2 \right)}{\left( \omega_{0j}^2 - \omega^2 \right)^2 + \gamma_j^2\omega^2}\]

    where the \( \omega_{0j} \) are the natural frequencies (“resonances”) of the electrons in the material.

    Normal dispersion. Away from a resonance, \( n \) rises with frequency. So in glass, blue light (higher frequency) is slower than red. That's why a prism splits white light into colours, and why simple lenses have coloured fringes (chromatic aberration).

    Anomalous dispersion. Near a resonance it's the other way round: \( n \) falls as frequency rises. The imaginary part of the permittivity peaks there too, meaning the material absorbs strongly. The two go together: a material that disperses must absorb somewhere, and a material that absorbs at some frequency must disperse near it.

    Two different speeds now matter:

    • Phase velocity \( \omega/k \): how fast the crests of a single frequency move. It can be faster than \( c \), for instance near a resonance.
    • Group velocity \( \dd{\omega}/\dd{k} \): how fast the overall shape of a pulse moves. In ordinary situations this is the speed at which energy and signals travel.

    Neither energy nor information ever travels faster than \( c \).

    Example#

    Find the skin depth in copper (\( \sigma = 6\times10^7\ \text{S/m} \)) at 60 Hz and at 1 GHz, and comment on the implications.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    For a good conductor,

    \[d = \frac{1}{\kappa} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\omega\mu\sigma}}\]

    with \( \mu \approx \mu_0 = 4\pi\times10^{-7} \).

    At 60 Hz (mains electricity), \( \omega = 2\pi(60) = 377\ \text{s}^{-1} \):

    \[d = \sqrt{\frac{2}{(377)(4\pi\times10^{-7})(6\times10^7)}} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{2.84\times10^4}} \approx 8.4\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}\]

    About 8 mm. A household wire is much thinner than that, so the current uses the whole wire. For very thick power-station bars, the effect starts to matter.

    At 1 GHz (microwaves, mobile phones), \( \omega = 6.28\times10^9\ \text{s}^{-1} \), which is \( 1.67\times10^7 \) times bigger. Since \( d \propto 1/\sqrt{\omega} \), the skin depth is \( \sqrt{1.67\times10^7} \approx 4100 \) times smaller:

    \[d \approx \frac{8.4\times10^{-3}}{4100} \approx 2.1\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}\]

    About two microns, much thinner than a human hair. At microwave frequencies the inside of a conductor carries no current and is wasted metal. That's why microwave parts can be made of cheap metal with a thin silver coating, and why hollow tubes work as well as solid rods.

    Example#

    For a medium with a single resonance, determine whether the index of refraction rises or falls with frequency well below resonance, and relate this to the behaviour of a glass prism.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Well below resonance (\( \omega \ll \omega_0 \)), the friction term is negligible, and the formula becomes

    \[n(\omega) \approx 1 + \frac{Nq^2 f}{2\epsilon_0 m}\frac{1}{\omega_0^2 - \omega^2}\]

    As \( \omega \) goes up towards \( \omega_0 \), the bottom \( \omega_0^2 - \omega^2 \) gets smaller, so the fraction gets bigger and \( n \) increases with frequency. That's normal dispersion.

    Glass has its resonances in the ultraviolet, so all visible light is below resonance and this applies. Blue light has a higher frequency than red, so a higher \( n \), so it travels more slowly.

    By Snell's law, a higher \( n \) means more bending. So at each face of a prism, blue bends more than red, and the colours fan out with blue bent the most. Rainbows work the same way (with water drops instead of a prism).

    This can't continue forever. Close to \( \omega_0 \), friction takes over, the material absorbs strongly, and \( n \) starts falling with frequency (anomalous dispersion). Glass is transparent to visible light precisely because its resonances are outside the visible range.

    Full derivation: §9.4.
