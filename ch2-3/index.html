    2.3 Electric Potential#

    Definition#

    Since \( \curl{\vec{E}} = \vec{0} \), the line integral of \( \vec{E} \) doesn't depend on the path, and so we can define a scalar potential:

    \[V(\vec{r}) \equiv -\int_{\mathcal{O}}^{\vec{r}} \vec{E} \cdot \dd{\vec{l}}\]

    Here \( \mathcal{O} \) is a reference point where \( V = 0 \), usually taken at infinity. The difference in potential between two points,

    \[V(\vec{b}) - V(\vec{a}) = -\int_{\vec{a}}^{\vec{b}} \vec{E} \cdot \dd{\vec{l}}\]

    doesn't depend on where you put the reference point. Going the other way, the gradient theorem gives

    \[\vec{E} = -\grad V\]

    So the field points “downhill” in potential, in the direction \( V \) drops fastest. The minus sign means a positive charge moves from high potential to low, just as a ball rolls downhill. The unit is the volt: one joule per coulomb.

    Potential is not the same as potential energy. Potential energy is potential times charge. \( V \) exists at every point, whether or not a charge is there.

    Why the potential is useful#

    \( \vec{E} \) needs three functions (its three components). \( V \) needs only one, yet it carries all the same information, because you get \( \vec{E} \) by differentiating. This is possible because the three components of \( \vec{E} \) are not independent: \( \curl{\vec{E}} = \vec{0} \) ties them together.

    Potentials also add, and since they are just numbers, you add them as plain numbers rather than as vectors.

    Poisson's and Laplace's equations#

    Put \( \vec{E} = -\grad V \) into Gauss's law:

    \[\div{\vec{E}} = \div{(-\grad V)} = -\laplacian V = \frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}\]

    This is Poisson's equation:

    \[\laplacian V = -\frac{\rho}{\epsilon_0}\]

    Where there is no charge it becomes Laplace's equation, \( \laplacian V = 0 \), the subject of Chapter 3.

    \( \curl{\vec{E}} = \vec{0} \) is automatically true for any \( \vec{E} \) made from a potential. So all of electrostatics boils down to this one equation for one unknown function.

    Potential of a localized distribution#

    For a point charge at the origin, integrating in from infinity gives \( V = q/4\pi\epsilon_0 r \). Adding up many charges,

    \[V(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int \frac{\rho(\vec{r'})}{\gr}\dd{\tau'}\]

    This integral is much easier than the one for \( \vec{E} \): there is no direction \( \vu{\gr} \) to keep track of, and the bottom has \( \gr \) rather than \( \gr^2 \). So the usual way to find a field is find \( V \) first, then differentiate.

    Boundary conditions#

    What happens to the field as you cross a sheet of charge \( \sigma \)?

    • Gauss's law, applied to a thin pillbox through the sheet, shows the part of \( \vec{E} \) perpendicular to the sheet jumps.
    • \( \oint \vec{E}\cdot\dd{\vec{l}} = 0 \), applied to a thin loop through the sheet, shows the part parallel to the sheet doesn't change.
    \[E_{\text{above}}^{\perp} - E_{\text{below}}^{\perp} = \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0}, \qquad \vec{E}_{\text{above}}^{\parallel} = \vec{E}_{\text{below}}^{\parallel}\]

    or in one line, \( \vec{E}{\text{above}} - \vec{E}{\text{below}} = \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0}\vu{n} \).

    The potential itself does not jump. \( \vec{E} \) stays finite, so integrating it across a sheet of zero thickness gives zero. But the slope of \( V \) does jump:

    \[\pdv{V_{\text{above}}}{n} - \pdv{V_{\text{below}}}{n} = -\frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0}\]

    Example#

    Find the potential inside and outside a spherical shell of radius \( R \) carrying uniform surface charge \( \sigma \), with total charge \( q \).

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    First the field, from Gauss's law. Outside, the shell acts like a point charge. Inside, a Gaussian sphere encloses no charge, so \( \vec{E} = \vec{0} \).

    Outside (\( r > R \)), integrate in from infinity:

    \[V(r) = -\int_\infty^r \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q}{r'^2}\dd{r'} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q}{r}\]

    Inside (\( r < R \)), the path from infinity has two parts. From \( \infty \) to \( R \) it's the same as above. From \( R \) inward the field is zero, so that part adds nothing:

    \[V(r) = -\int_\infty^R \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q}{r'^2}\dd{r'} - \int_R^r 0 \dd{r'} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q}{R}\]

    So inside, the potential is a constant, equal to its value at the surface. It is not zero. Zero field means the potential is flat, not that it is zero. (Think of a flat plateau high on a mountain: no slope, but plenty of height.) Mixing these up is a very common mistake.

    The potential is continuous at \( r = R \), as it must be, while its slope jumps there because of the surface charge.

    Example#

    Find the potential a distance \( z \) above the centre of a uniformly charged disc of radius \( R \) and surface charge \( \sigma \), then obtain the field.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Cut the disc into thin rings of radius \( s \) and width \( \dd{s} \). Each ring has charge \( \dd{q} = \sigma, 2\pi s \dd{s} \), and every point of it is the same distance \( \gr = \sqrt{s^2 + z^2} \) from the field point. So each ring's potential is simple, and we just add up the rings:

    \[V = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_0^R \frac{\sigma\, 2\pi s \dd{s}}{\sqrt{s^2+z^2}} = \frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}\int_0^R \frac{s \dd{s}}{\sqrt{s^2+z^2}}\]

    Substituting \( u = s^2 + z^2 \) makes this easy:

    \[V = \frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}\left[ \sqrt{s^2+z^2} \right]_0^R = \frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}\left( \sqrt{R^2+z^2} - z \right)\]

    (for \( z > 0 \)). Now differentiate to get the field:

    \[E_z = -\pdv{V}{z} = \frac{\sigma}{2\epsilon_0}\left( 1 - \frac{z}{\sqrt{R^2+z^2}} \right)\]

    Check the limits. For a huge disc (\( R \to \infty \)) this becomes \( \sigma/2\epsilon_0 \), the infinite-plane field. Far away (\( z \gg R \)), expanding the square root gives \( Q/4\pi\epsilon_0 z^2 \) with \( Q = \sigma\pi R^2 \): a point charge.

    Finding \( V \) first and then differentiating avoided all the vector components that a direct calculation of \( \vec{E} \) would need.

    Full derivation: §2.3.
