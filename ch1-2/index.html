    1.2 Differential Calculus#

    An ordinary derivative \( df/dx \) tells you how fast \( f \) changes as you move along a line.

    In three dimensions one number is not enough, because the rate of change depends on which direction you move. The fix is the “del” operator:

    \[\grad = \vu{x} \pdv{}{x} + \vu{y} \pdv{}{y} + \vu{z} \pdv{}{z}\]

    Del is not really a vector. On its own it means nothing; it is an instruction to differentiate whatever comes after it. But it behaves like a vector, so it can act in the same three ways a vector can multiply:

    • on a scalar → the gradient \( \grad t \)
    • with a dot product on a vector → the divergence \( \div{\vec{v}} \)
    • with a cross product on a vector → the curl \( \curl{\vec{v}} \)

    Maxwell's equations are four statements about the divergence and curl of \( \vec{E} \) and \( \vec{B} \). So these three derivatives are all the calculus the subject needs.

    Gradient#

    For a scalar field \( t(x,y,z) \) (say, the temperature at each point in a room),

    \[\grad t = \pdv{t}{x}\vu{x} + \pdv{t}{y}\vu{y} + \pdv{t}{z}\vu{z}\]

    When you take a small step \( \dd{\vec{l}} \), the change in \( t \) is

    \[\dd{t} = (\grad t) \cdot \dd{\vec{l}} = |\grad t| \, |\dd{\vec{l}}| \cos\theta\]

    This tells you what the gradient means. The change is biggest when \( \theta = 0 \), that is, when you step along \( \grad t \). So:

    • the gradient points in the direction in which \( t \) increases fastest, and
    • its size is the slope in that direction.

    Picture standing on a hillside. The gradient of the height points straight uphill, and its size is how steep that is. If you step at right angles to the gradient, \( \dd{t} = 0 \): you walk along a contour line. So the gradient is always perpendicular to surfaces of constant \( t \).

    Where the gradient is zero the ground is flat: a hilltop, a valley bottom or a saddle (a mountain pass).

    Divergence#

    \[\div{\vec{v}} = \pdv{v_x}{x} + \pdv{v_y}{y} + \pdv{v_z}{z}\]

    A scalar that measures how much the field spreads out from a point. Imagine \( \vec{v} \) is the velocity of water on a pond, and you sprinkle sawdust on it. If the sawdust spreads apart, you are at a point of positive divergence (a source, like a tap). If it bunches together, the divergence is negative (a sink, like a drain). Water that neither spreads nor bunches has zero divergence.

    Curl#

    \[\curl{\vec{v}} = \begin{vmatrix} \vu{x} & \vu{y} & \vu{z} \\ \pdv{}{x} & \pdv{}{y} & \pdv{}{z} \\ v_x & v_y & v_z \end{vmatrix}\]

    A vector that measures how much the field swirls around a point. Put a small paddle wheel in the water: if it spins, the curl is non-zero there. The curl points along the wheel's axle, in the direction given by the right-hand rule. A whirlpool is a region of large curl.

    Careful: you can't always see divergence or curl just by looking.

    Product rules#

    As with ordinary derivatives, there are product rules. There are six; these five are the ones used most (the sixth, for \( \curl{(\vec{A} \cross \vec{B})} \), is in the full notes):

    \[\grad(fg) = f(\grad g) + g(\grad f)\] \[\grad(\vec{A} \cdot \vec{B}) = \vec{A} \cross (\curl{\vec{B}}) + \vec{B} \cross (\curl{\vec{A}}) + (\vec{A} \cdot \grad)\vec{B} + (\vec{B} \cdot \grad)\vec{A}\] \[\div{(f\vec{A})} = f(\div{\vec{A}}) + \vec{A} \cdot (\grad f)\] \[\div{(\vec{A} \cross \vec{B})} = \vec{B} \cdot (\curl{\vec{A}}) - \vec{A} \cdot (\curl{\vec{B}})\] \[\curl{(f\vec{A})} = f(\curl{\vec{A}}) - \vec{A} \cross (\grad f)\]

    Each one can be proved by writing out the components and using the ordinary product rule.

    Second derivatives#

    Applying \( \grad \) twice gives five possible second derivatives. Two of them are always zero:

    \[\div{(\curl{\vec{v}})} = 0, \qquad \curl{(\grad t)} = \vec{0}\]

    An easy way to remember why: they look like \( \vec{A} \cdot (\vec{A} \cross \vec{B}) \) and \( \vec{A} \cross \vec{A} \), with \( \grad \) in place of \( \vec{A} \), and both of those are zero. (Intuitively: a pure swirl has no source, and walking uphill round a closed loop can't produce any circulation.)

    These two facts are the foundation of potentials. In electrostatics \( \curl{\vec{E}} = \vec{0} \), so \( \vec{E} \) can be written as a gradient: that gives the electric potential. And \( \div{\vec{B}} = 0 \) always, so \( \vec{B} \) can be written as a curl: that gives the vector potential. §1.6 makes this precise.

    Of the other three, the important one is the Laplacian, the divergence of the gradient:

    \[\laplacian t = \div{(\grad t)} = \pdv{^2 t}{x^2} + \pdv{^2 t}{y^2} + \pdv{^2 t}{z^2}\]

    The second is the gradient of the divergence, \( \grad(\div{\vec{v}}) \). It has no special name and turns up less often. Note that it is not the same as the Laplacian of a vector, \( \laplacian \vec{v} \) (which means the Laplacian of each component).

    The third, the curl of the curl, is not new; it breaks down into the other two:

    \[\curl{(\curl{\vec{v}})} = \grad(\div{\vec{v}}) - \laplacian \vec{v}\]

    You will use this identity to get the wave equation out of Maxwell's equations (§9.2).

    So there are really just two independent second derivatives: the Laplacian (very important) and the gradient of the divergence (seldom needed).

    Example#

    Compute the divergence of \( \vec{v}_1 = \vu{r} \) and of \( \vec{v}_2 = \vu{r}/r^2 \) at points away from the origin. Both fields point radially outward; account for the difference in the results.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    In spherical coordinates (§1.4), the divergence of a field that points only radially is

    \[\div{\vec{v}} = \frac{1}{r^2}\pdv{}{r}\left( r^2 v_r \right)\]

    For \( \vec{v}_1 = \vu{r} \), \( v_r = 1 \):

    \[\div{\vec{v}_1} = \frac{1}{r^2}\pdv{}{r}(r^2) = \frac{2}{r}\]

    For \( \vec{v}_2 = \vu{r}/r^2 \), \( v_r = 1/r^2 \), so \( r^2 v_r = 1 \) is a constant and

    \[\div{\vec{v}_2} = \frac{1}{r^2}\pdv{}{r}(1) = 0 \qquad (r \neq 0)\]

    A sketch won't show the difference: both sets of arrows point outward. Think of the flow through spheres of growing radius. A sphere's area grows like \( r^2 \). The first field keeps the same strength as the spheres grow, so more and more flows out through bigger spheres: something must be adding to the flow, and the divergence is positive. The second field weakens like \( 1/r^2 \), exactly cancelling the growth in area, so the same amount flows through every sphere: nothing is added between them, and the divergence is zero.

    The second field is the field of a point charge. It has zero divergence everywhere except the origin, yet a non-zero flow through any sphere around the origin. That puzzle is solved by the Dirac delta function in §1.5.

    Example#

    Determine the curl of \( \vec{v} = -y\vu{x} + x\vu{y} \) and of \( \vec{w} = x\vu{x} + y\vu{y} \).

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Both fields lie in the \( xy \) plane and don't depend on \( z \), so only the \( z \) component of the curl is non-zero:

    \[(\curl{\vec{v}})_z = \pdv{v_y}{x} - \pdv{v_x}{y}\]

    For \( \vec{v} = -y\vu{x} + x\vu{y} \):

    \[\curl{\vec{v}} = \left[ \pdv{}{x}(x) - \pdv{}{y}(-y) \right]\vu{z} = (1 + 1)\vu{z} = 2\vu{z}\]

    This field goes round the origin anticlockwise, and the curl points along \( +\vu{z} \), as the right-hand rule says. Its divergence is zero.

    For \( \vec{w} = x\vu{x} + y\vu{y} \):

    \[\curl{\vec{w}} = \left[ \pdv{}{x}(y) - \pdv{}{y}(x) \right]\vu{z} = \vec{0}\]

    This field points straight outward and doesn't swirl. Its divergence is \( \div{\vec{w}} = 2 \).

    The pair shows that divergence and curl measure different things: the first field swirls but doesn't spread, the second spreads but doesn't swirl.

    Full derivation: §1.2.
