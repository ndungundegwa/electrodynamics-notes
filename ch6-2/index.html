    6.2 The Field of a Magnetized Object#

    Bound currents#

    If you know the magnetization \( \vec{M} \), you can get the vector potential by adding up all the tiny dipoles:

    \[\vec{A}(\vec{r}) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int \frac{\vec{M}(\vec{r'})\cross\vu{\gr}}{\gr^2}\dd{\tau'}\]

    Integrating by parts, just as in §4.2, splits this into a volume term and a surface term:

    \[\vec{A}(\vec{r}) = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\int_V \frac{\curl{\vec{M}}}{\gr}\dd{\tau'} + \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\oint_S \frac{\vec{M}\cross\vu{n}}{\gr}\dd{a'}\]

    These are the potentials of a volume current and a surface current:

    \[\vec{J}_b = \curl{\vec{M}}, \qquad \vec{K}_b = \vec{M}\cross\vu{n}\]

    called the bound currents. So a magnetized object makes exactly the same field as these two ordinary currents, and everything from Chapter 5 applies.

    What the bound currents really are#

    Bound currents are real flows of charge.

    Surface current. Think of the magnetized material as a tightly packed grid of tiny current loops, all circulating the same way. Where two neighbouring loops touch, their currents run in opposite directions and cancel. At the outer edge there is no neighbour to cancel with, so a current is left flowing round the surface. (Like a crowd of people all walking in small clockwise circles: in the middle they cancel out, but round the edge there's a net flow.) Its size is \( |\vec{M}\cross\vu{n}| \). It's zero where \( \vec{M} \) points straight out of the surface, because then the loops lie flat in the surface and no current crosses the edge.

    Volume current. If the magnetization is uneven, neighbouring loops don't quite cancel: a stronger loop next to a weaker one leaves a bit of current over. The curl measures exactly this leftover.

    Bound currents always have \( \div{\vec{J}_b} = 0 \) (the divergence of a curl is zero). That has to be so: they flow in closed loops and charge never piles up anywhere.

    Comparison with bound charges#

    ElectricMagnetic
    Volume\( \rho_b = -\div{\vec{P}} \)\( \vec{J}_b = \curl{\vec{M}} \)
    Surface\( \sigma_b = \vec{P}\cdot\vu{n} \)\( \vec{K}_b = \vec{M}\cross\vu{n} \)

    The same pattern as before: divergence and dot product for electric, curl and cross product for magnetic.

    Example#

    Find the field of a uniformly magnetized sphere of radius \( R \), with \( \vec{M} = M\vu{z} \).

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    The magnetization is uniform, so \( \curl{\vec{M}} = \vec{0} \): no bound volume current. The bound surface current is

    \[\vec{K}_b = \vec{M}\cross\vu{n} = M\vu{z}\cross\vu{r} = M\sin\theta\,\vu{\phi}\]

    It flows round the sphere like lines of latitude, strongest at the equator and zero at the poles.

    That is exactly the current on a spinning charged spherical shell. From §5.4, a shell with charge \( \sigma \) spinning at \( \omega \) has \( K = \sigma\omega R\sin\theta \). So we can reuse that answer with \( \sigma\omega R \) replaced by \( M \).

    The field of a spinning shell is uniform inside and a pure dipole outside:

    \[\vec{B}_{\text{in}} = \frac{2}{3}\mu_0 \vec{M}\]

    and outside, the field of a dipole with total moment

    \[\vec{m} = \frac{4}{3}\pi R^3 \vec{M}\]

    which is magnetization × volume.

    Notice that \( \vec{B} \) inside points along \( \vec{M} \). The electrically polarized sphere of §4.2 had \( \vec{E} = -\vec{P}/3\epsilon_0 \) inside, pointing against \( \vec{P} \). The electric and magnetic cases are very similar, but not identical, and this sign is where they differ. (§6.3 explains why.)

    Example#

    An infinite cylinder of radius \( R \) carries magnetization \( \vec{M} = k s^2 \vu{\phi} \), where \( s \) is the distance from the axis. Find the bound currents and the resulting field.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Volume current. \( \vec{M} \) only circles the axis and only depends on \( s \), so the curl in cylindrical coordinates is

    \[\vec{J}_b = \curl{\vec{M}} = \frac{1}{s}\pdv{}{s}\left( s M_\phi \right)\vu{z} = \frac{1}{s}\pdv{}{s}\left( k s^3 \right)\vu{z} = 3ks\,\vu{z}\]

    This flows along the cylinder, more strongly further from the axis.

    Surface current. On the curved surface \( \vu{n} = \vu{s} \), so

    \[\vec{K}_b = \vec{M}\cross\vu{n} = kR^2\left( \vu{\phi}\cross\vu{s} \right) = -kR^2\,\vu{z}\]

    flowing the opposite way, back down the outside.

    Check: bound currents form closed loops, so the total should be zero:

    \[\int \vec{J}_b \cdot \dd{\vec{a}} + \oint K_b \dd{l} = \int_0^R (3ks)2\pi s\dd{s} - kR^2(2\pi R) = 2\pi k R^3 - 2\pi k R^3 = 0\]

    ✓

    Field. Use Ampère's law on a circle of radius \( s < R \) around the axis. The current through it is \( \int_0^s 3ks',2\pi s' \dd{s'} = 2\pi k s^3 \):

    \[B(s)\,2\pi s = \mu_0 2\pi k s^3 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \vec{B} = \mu_0 k s^2 \vu{\phi} = \mu_0\vec{M}\]

    Outside, the total current through the loop is zero, so \( \vec{B} = \vec{0} \), which fits with \( \vec{M} \) being zero there too.

    Full derivation: §6.2.
