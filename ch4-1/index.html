    4.1 Polarization#

    Dielectrics#

    Chapters 2 and 3 were about conductors, where charge moves freely. This chapter is about dielectrics: insulators such as glass, plastic or water, where every electron stays attached to its own atom. These bound charges can't travel through the material. But they can shift a tiny bit inside each atom or molecule, and those tiny shifts are enough to change the field a lot.

    This happens in two ways.

    1. Stretched atoms (induced dipoles)#

    Put a neutral atom in an electric field. The field pushes the nucleus one way and the electron cloud the other, until the attraction between them balances the push. The atom is now slightly stretched, with a little \( + \) end and a little \( - \) end: an induced dipole. For any field you can make in a lab, the stretch is proportional to the field:

    \[\vec{p} = \alpha \vec{E}\]

    \( \alpha \) is the atomic polarizability, a number that describes how easily that kind of atom stretches.

    Molecules may stretch more easily in some directions than others. Then \( \alpha \) becomes a matrix (the polarizability tensor), and \( \vec{p} \) need not point exactly along \( \vec{E} \).

    2. Turning molecules (polar molecules)#

    Some molecules, such as water, have a built-in dipole moment even with no field. In a uniform field the pushes on the two ends are equal and opposite, so there's no net force. But there is a twist (torque):

    \[\vec{N} = \vec{p} \cross \vec{E}\]

    which turns the molecule to line up with the field, like a compass needle. Random thermal jiggling fights against this, so the molecules only partly line up, and less so when the material is hotter.

    If the field is not uniform, the two ends feel different fields, so there is also a net force:

    \[\vec{F} = (\vec{p}\cdot\grad)\vec{E}\]

    This is why a charged rod picks up uncharged bits of paper. The rod's field polarizes the paper, and since the field is stronger nearer the rod, the dipoles get pulled towards it.

    Polarization#

    Either way, the material ends up full of tiny dipoles lined up with the field. We describe this with the polarization:

    \[\vec{P} \equiv \text{dipole moment per unit volume}\]

    with units C/m². From here on we only need \( \vec{P} \); the details of the atoms don't matter any more. §4.2 works out the field that a polarized object produces.

    Example#

    A point charge \( q \) is a distance \( r \) from a neutral atom of polarizability \( \alpha \). Find the force between them and its dependence on \( r \).

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    The charge's field at the atom has size

    \[E = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2}\]

    This stretches the atom into a dipole \( p = \alpha E \), pointing along the line between them (away from \( q \) if \( q \) is positive).

    A point charge's field is not uniform, so we need the non-uniform force formula. With the dipole along the radial direction,

    \[F = p \left| \dv{E}{r} \right| = \alpha E \left| \dv{}{r}\left( \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^2} \right) \right| = \alpha E \frac{2q}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^3}\]

    Put in \( E \):

    \[F = 2\alpha \left( \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0} \right)^2 \frac{1}{r^5}\]

    The force is always attractive, whatever the sign of \( q \). Flip the charge and the induced dipole flips too, so the two sign changes cancel. It falls off as \( 1/r^5 \), far faster than the \( 1/r^2 \) between two charges. That's why forces from polarization only matter at short range.

    Example#

    An ideal dipole \( \vec{p} \) is placed in a uniform field \( \vec{E} \) at angle \( \theta \) to it. Find the energy of the configuration and the stable orientation.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    The torque is \( N = pE\sin\theta \), trying to make \( \theta \) smaller. The work needed to turn the dipole against it, from \( \theta_0 \) to \( \theta \), is

    \[W = \int_{\theta_0}^{\theta} pE\sin\theta' \dd{\theta'} = -pE\left( \cos\theta - \cos\theta_0 \right)\]

    Choose zero energy at \( \theta_0 = 90^\circ \), where \( \cos\theta_0 = 0 \). Then

    \[U = -pE\cos\theta = -\vec{p}\cdot\vec{E}\]

    The energy is lowest at \( \theta = 0 \), dipole along the field: this is the stable position. It is highest at \( \theta = 180^\circ \), pointing against the field. That is also a balance point, but an unstable one, like a pencil standing on its tip.

    The energy difference between the two is \( 2pE \). Molecules only line up fully if this is much bigger than the thermal energy \( k_B T \). At room temperature, with ordinary lab fields, it isn't: thermal motion keeps the alignment small. That's why the polarization of materials like water stays proportional to the field.

    Full derivation: §4.1.
