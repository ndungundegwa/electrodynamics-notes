    3.4 Multipole Expansion#

    Approximate potentials far away#

    From far enough away, any small blob of charge looks like a point charge. The multipole expansion turns that into a precise series: a point-charge term, then smaller and smaller corrections in powers of \( 1/r \).

    Start from \( V = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int \rho(\vec{r'})/\gr, \dd{\tau'} \). With \( \alpha \) the angle between \( \vec{r} \) and \( \vec{r'} \), the cosine rule gives

    \[\gr = r\sqrt{1 + \left( \frac{r'}{r} \right)^2 - 2\frac{r'}{r}\cos\alpha}\]

    Far away, \( r'/r \) is small, so expand \( 1/\gr \) in powers of it. The coefficients turn out to be Legendre polynomials:

    \[\frac{1}{\gr} = \frac{1}{r}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left( \frac{r'}{r} \right)^n P_n(\cos\alpha)\]

    This gives the multipole expansion of the potential:

    \[V(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{r^{n+1}}\int (r')^n P_n(\cos\alpha)\rho(\vec{r'})\dd{\tau'}\]

    The first few terms#

    The first three terms are called monopole, dipole and quadrupole:

    \[V_{\text{mon}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{Q}{r}, \qquad V_{\text{dip}} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{\vec{p}\cdot\vu{r}}{r^2}, \qquad V_{\text{quad}} \sim \frac{1}{r^3}\]

    Here \( Q = \int \rho \dd{\tau} \) is the total charge and

    \[\vec{p} = \int \vec{r'}\rho(\vec{r'})\dd{\tau'}\]

    is the dipole moment. For point charges, \( \vec{p} = \sum q_i \vec{r}_i \). For a pair \( +q \) and \( -q \) separated by \( \vec{d} \), it is \( \vec{p} = q\vec{d} \), pointing from the negative charge to the positive one.

    Far away, the first term that isn't zero wins. If the total charge isn't zero, the object looks like a point charge. If it is neutral, the dipole term takes over, falling as \( 1/r^2 \). If the dipole moment is zero too, the quadrupole term leads, and so on.

    Does the origin matter?#

    The total charge \( Q \) doesn't depend on where you put the origin. The dipole moment usually does: moving the origin by \( \vec{a} \) gives

    \[\vec{p}_{\text{new}} = \vec{p} - Q\vec{a}\]

    So \( \vec{p} \) is the same for every origin exactly when the total charge is zero. In general, the first moment that isn't zero never depends on the origin.

    The field of a dipole#

    With \( \vec{p} \) along the \( z \)-axis, take the gradient of the dipole potential in spherical coordinates:

    \[E_r = \frac{2p\cos\theta}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^3}, \qquad E_\theta = \frac{p\sin\theta}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^3}, \qquad E_\phi = 0\]

    or all together,

    \[\vec{E}_{\text{dip}}(\vec{r}) = \frac{p}{4\pi\epsilon_0 r^3}\left( 2\cos\theta\,\vu{r} + \sin\theta\,\vu{\theta} \right)\]

    A dipole's field falls as \( 1/r^3 \), one power faster than a point charge's. The \( + \) and \( - \) charges almost cancel from far away. In general the \( n \)th term's field falls as \( 1/r^{n+2} \).

    Example#

    Four charges sit in the \( xy \) plane: \( +q \) at \( (a,0) \) and \( (-a,0) \), and \( -q \) at \( (0,a) \) and \( (0,-a) \). Find the leading term in the multipole expansion.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Monopole. The total charge is \( q + q - q - q = 0 \), so there is no monopole term, and the potential falls faster than \( 1/r \).

    Dipole. Add up \( \vec{p} = \sum q_i \vec{r}_i \):

    \[\vec{p} = q(a\vu{x}) + q(-a\vu{x}) + (-q)(a\vu{y}) + (-q)(-a\vu{y}) = \vec{0}\]

    Each pair cancels, so the dipole moment is zero too. The total charge is zero, so this is true wherever you put the origin.

    Quadrupole. With the first two terms gone, the quadrupole term leads. The potential falls as \( 1/r^3 \) and the field as \( 1/r^4 \).

    You can think of this arrangement as two dipoles pointing in opposite directions, side by side. Their dipole moments cancel, but because they are not in the same place, their fields don't cancel completely. What's left is a quadrupole.

    Example#

    A sphere of radius \( R \) carries surface charge \( \sigma(\theta) = k\cos\theta \), with \( k \) constant. Find the potential outside.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Monopole. The total charge is

    \[Q = \int \sigma \dd{a} = k R^2 \int_0^{2\pi}\!\!\int_0^\pi \cos\theta \sin\theta \dd{\theta}\dd{\phi} = 2\pi k R^2 \int_0^\pi \cos\theta\sin\theta \dd{\theta} = 0\]

    The top half is positive and the bottom half negative, and they cancel.

    Dipole. By symmetry \( \vec{p} \) points along \( \vu{z} \). Use \( z' = R\cos\theta \):

    \[p_z = \int z' \sigma \dd{a} = \int (R\cos\theta)(k\cos\theta)R^2\sin\theta \dd{\theta}\dd{\phi}\] \[= 2\pi k R^3 \int_0^\pi \cos^2\theta \sin\theta \dd{\theta} = 2\pi k R^3 \cdot \frac{2}{3} = \frac{4\pi k R^3}{3}\]

    So the dipole term leads:

    \[V(r,\theta) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{p\cos\theta}{r^2} = \frac{kR^3\cos\theta}{3\epsilon_0 r^2}\]

    In fact this is the exact answer, not just an approximation. The charge goes as \( \cos\theta = P_1(\cos\theta) \), so in the method of §3.3 only the \( l=1 \) term appears. All the higher terms are exactly zero.
