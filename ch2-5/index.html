    2.5 Conductors#

    Basic properties#

    A conductor (such as a metal) has charges that are free to move around. Treat the supply as unlimited. Five properties follow, and they all come from the first.

    1. \( \vec{E} = \vec{0} \) inside. If there were a field inside, it would push the free charges. They would keep moving until their new arrangement cancelled the field. Once everything has settled, no field is left.

    2. \( \rho = 0 \) inside. Gauss's law gives \( \rho = \epsilon_0 \div{\vec{E}} = 0 \).

    3. Any net charge sits on the surface. There's nowhere else for it to go.

    4. The whole conductor is at one potential. With \( \vec{E} = \vec{0} \) inside, \( V(b) - V(a) = -\int \vec{E}\cdot\dd{\vec{l}} = 0 \) between any two points in it.

    5. Just outside, \( \vec{E} \) is perpendicular to the surface. If it had a part along the surface, that part would push the surface charges along until it was gone.

    Induced charges#

    Bring a positive charge near an uncharged conductor. Negative charge is pulled to the near side and positive charge pushed to the far side. The conductor is still neutral overall, but its charges have separated. The nearby negative charge is closer than the far positive charge, so the net force is attractive. That's why a charged comb picks up bits of paper.

    Put a charge \( q \) inside a hollow in a conductor. Then \( -q \) collects on the hollow's wall and \( +q \) on the outer surface. Why: draw a Gaussian surface inside the metal around the hollow. \( \vec{E} = \vec{0} \) there, so the charge enclosed must be zero, and the wall must carry \( -q \) to cancel \( q \).

    An empty hollow has zero field inside, whatever is going on outside. So a conductor shields its inside from outside fields. This is the Faraday cage, and it's why your phone loses signal in a lift. It doesn't work the other way round: a charge inside the hollow still produces a field outside (unless the conductor is connected to ground).

    Surface charge and the force on a conductor#

    Just outside the surface the field points straight out, and (using the boundary condition of §2.3 with zero field inside)

    \[\vec{E} = \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0}\vu{n}\]

    The force per unit area on the surface charge is not simply \( \sigma E \). A patch of charge can't push on itself, and part of that field is its own. The right field to use is the average of the fields just inside (zero) and just outside:

    \[\vec{f} = \sigma \vec{E}_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\sigma^2}{2\epsilon_0}\vu{n}\]

    This electrostatic pressure always pulls the surface outward, whatever the sign of the charge. It can also be written \( P = \epsilon_0 E^2 / 2 \).

    Capacitors#

    Put \( +Q \) on one conductor and \( -Q \) on another. Double \( Q \) and the field doubles everywhere, so the voltage between them doubles too. So \( V \) is proportional to \( Q \), and the ratio is the capacitance:

    \[C \equiv \frac{Q}{V}\]

    measured in farads. It depends only on the shapes, sizes and spacing of the conductors. Standard results:

    \[C_{\text{parallel plate}} = \frac{\epsilon_0 A}{d}, \qquad C_{\text{sphere}} = 4\pi\epsilon_0 R, \qquad C_{\text{coaxial}} = \frac{2\pi\epsilon_0 L}{\ln(b/a)}\]

    To charge a capacitor you move charge \( \dd{q} \) across a voltage \( q/C \) that grows as you go. The total work is

    \[W = \int_0^Q \frac{q}{C}\dd{q} = \frac{1}{2}\frac{Q^2}{C} = \frac{1}{2}CV^2\]

    Example#

    A point charge \( q \) sits at the centre of a spherical conducting shell with inner radius \( a \) and outer radius \( b \), carrying no net charge. Find the field in all regions and the induced surface charges.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Region \( r < a \). Only \( q \) is enclosed, so Gauss's law gives

    \[\vec{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2}\vu{r}\]

    Region \( a < r < b \) (inside the metal): \( \vec{E} = \vec{0} \). A Gaussian sphere here must enclose zero charge. The point charge gives \( q \), so the inner surface must carry

    \[q_{\text{inner}} = -q, \qquad \sigma_{\text{inner}} = \frac{-q}{4\pi a^2}\]

    Region \( r > b \). The shell has no net charge, so the outer surface carries \( +q \) to balance the \( -q \) on the inner surface:

    \[q_{\text{outer}} = +q, \qquad \sigma_{\text{outer}} = \frac{q}{4\pi b^2}\]

    The total enclosed is \( q - q + q = q \), so

    \[\vec{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q}{r^2}\vu{r}\]

    That's the same as if the shell weren't there. From outside, you can't tell the shell exists. Even if the point charge were off-centre, the inner surface charge would be lopsided, but the outer charge would still spread out evenly, and the outside field would be unchanged.

    Example#

    Find the capacitance of a parallel-plate capacitor of plate area \( A \) and separation \( d \), and the energy stored at potential difference \( V \).

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Put \( +Q \) on one plate and \( -Q \) on the other, and ignore the edges. The surface charge is \( \sigma = Q/A \). From §2.2 the field between the plates is uniform:

    \[E = \frac{\sigma}{\epsilon_0} = \frac{Q}{\epsilon_0 A}\]

    The field is constant, so the voltage is just field × distance:

    \[V = Ed = \frac{Qd}{\epsilon_0 A}\]

    So

    \[C = \frac{Q}{V} = \frac{\epsilon_0 A}{d}\]

    It depends only on the geometry, as expected. Bigger plates or a smaller gap give a bigger capacitance. The stored energy is \( W = \frac{1}{2}CV^2 = \epsilon_0 A V^2 / 2d \).

    Check with the field energy (§2.4). The energy density is \( \epsilon_0 E^2/2 \) and the field fills a volume \( Ad \), giving \( \frac{\epsilon_0}{2}(V/d)^2 Ad = \epsilon_0 A V^2/2d \). It agrees. ✓

    Full derivation: §2.5.
