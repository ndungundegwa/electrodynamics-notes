    12.3 Relativistic Electrodynamics#

    Magnetism as a relativistic phenomenon#

    Magnetism isn't really a separate force. It is what electric forces look like when charges move and you take relativity into account.

    Take a wire carrying a current: positive ions sitting still, and negative electrons drifting along, in equal amounts so the wire is neutral. A test charge moves alongside the wire. In the lab the wire is neutral, so there's no electric field, and the force on the test charge is purely magnetic.

    Now look from the test charge's point of view. The ions and electrons now move at different speeds than before, so they are Lorentz-contracted by different amounts. One set gets squeezed closer together than the other, so the wire is no longer neutral: it has a net charge. And the test charge is now at rest, so the force on it must be purely electric.

    Both observers agree there's a force and agree on what it does. They just disagree about whether to call it electric or magnetic. Magnetism is the relativistic correction to Coulomb's law for moving charges.

    Electrons in a wire drift at less than a millimetre per second, so the correction is incredibly small (it involves \( v^2/c^2 \)). How can it cause forces big enough to run a motor? Because the electric forces between all the charges in a wire are enormous, and normally cancel almost perfectly. Even a minute imbalance in such huge forces is easy to notice.

    How the fields transform#

    \( \vec{E} \) and \( \vec{B} \) are not two separate things. They are parts of one object, the electromagnetic field. Switching to a frame moving at speed \( v \) along \( x \) mixes them:

    \[\bar{E}_x = E_x, \qquad \bar{E}_y = \gamma\left( E_y - vB_z \right), \qquad \bar{E}_z = \gamma\left( E_z + vB_y \right)\] \[\bar{B}_x = B_x, \qquad \bar{B}_y = \gamma\left( B_y + \frac{v}{c^2}E_z \right), \qquad \bar{B}_z = \gamma\left( B_z - \frac{v}{c^2}E_y \right)\]

    Parts along the direction of motion don't change; the perpendicular parts mix. A purely electric field in one frame has a magnetic part in another, and vice versa. So “is this field electric or magnetic?” has no single answer; it depends who's looking.

    But two combinations are the same for everyone:

    \[E^2 - c^2B^2, \qquad \vec{E}\cdot\vec{B}\]

    So if the fields are perpendicular in one frame, they're perpendicular in every frame. And if \( E > cB \) in one frame, no observer can see a purely magnetic field.

    The field tensor#

    The way the fields mix is exactly how a 4×4 antisymmetric matrix (a second-rank tensor) transforms:

    \[F^{\mu\nu} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & E_x/c & E_y/c & E_z/c \\ -E_x/c & 0 & B_z & -B_y \\ -E_y/c & -B_z & 0 & B_x \\ -E_z/c & B_y & -B_x & 0 \end{pmatrix}\]

    It has six independent entries (three from \( \vec{E} \) and three from \( \vec{B} \)), together making one object: the field tensor. A companion, the dual tensor \( G^{\mu\nu} \), is obtained by the substitution \( \vec{E}/c \to \vec{B} \), \( \vec{B} \to -\vec{E}/c \).

    Electrodynamics in tensor notation#

    Charge density and current combine into a four-vector \( J^\mu = (c\rho, \vec{J}) \). Then all four of Maxwell's equations shrink to just two:

    \[\partial_\mu F^{\mu\nu} = \mu_0 J^\nu, \qquad \partial_\mu G^{\mu\nu} = 0\]

    The first contains Gauss's law and the Ampère–Maxwell law; the second contains \( \div{\vec{B}} = 0 \) and Faraday's law. Conservation of charge drops straight out of the first, because \( F^{\mu\nu} \) is antisymmetric.

    The Lorentz force law becomes

    \[\dv{p^\mu}{\bar{t}} = q\eta_\nu F^{\mu\nu}\]

    Written like this, it's obvious the theory works the same in every frame (it's manifestly covariant): each equation says one tensor equals another of the same kind, so if it's true in one frame it's true in all. Electrodynamics never needed fixing for relativity. Maxwell's equations (1865) were already relativistic 40 years before Einstein (1905). That's exactly why they clashed with Newtonian mechanics, and it was Newtonian mechanics that had to change.

    Relativistic potentials#

    The scalar and vector potentials also combine into a four-vector,

    \[A^\mu = \left( \frac{V}{c}, \vec{A} \right)\]

    in terms of which \( F^{\mu\nu} = \partial^\mu A^\nu - \partial^\nu A^\mu \), and the Lorenz gauge condition of §10.1 becomes simply

    \[\partial_\mu A^\mu = 0\]

    which clearly means the same in every frame. In this gauge, the wave equations for the potentials become the single equation \( \Box^2 A^\mu = -\mu_0 J^\mu \).

    That's why the Lorenz gauge is preferred over the Coulomb gauge: it treats all frames equally, whereas \( \div{\vec{A}} = 0 \) is only true in one particular frame.

    Example#

    A point charge is at rest in frame \( \bar{S} \), which moves at speed \( v \) along \( x \) relative to the lab. Find the fields in the lab frame and describe their shape.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    In \( \bar{S} \) the charge is at rest, so there's just the ordinary Coulomb field and \( \bar{\vec{B}} = \vec{0} \). Transform back to the lab, and the magnetic field is no longer zero:

    \[B_y = -\frac{\gamma v}{c^2}\bar{E}_z, \qquad B_z = \frac{\gamma v}{c^2}\bar{E}_y\]

    which can be written compactly as

    \[\vec{B} = \frac{1}{c^2}\left( \vec{v}\cross\vec{E} \right)\]

    A moving charge makes a magnetic field circling its path. At low speed this is the Biot-Savart result, and we got it just by looking at a static Coulomb field from a moving frame.

    Carrying out the transformation of the electric field gives

    \[\vec{E} = \frac{q}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{1-v^2/c^2}{\left( 1 - \frac{v^2}{c^2}\sin^2\theta \right)^{3/2}}\frac{\vu{R}}{R^2}\]

    pointing away from where the charge is now, with \( \theta \) measured from the direction of motion. That's exactly the result of §10.3, which took pages of Liénard-Wiechert algebra. Here it takes a few lines.

    The shape is a pancake: weaker in front and behind, stronger to the sides, and as \( v \to c \) it flattens into a disc at right angles to the motion. That's just the field pattern being Lorentz-contracted like everything else.

    Example#

    A field has \( \vec{E} \) and \( \vec{B} \) perpendicular, with \( E < cB \). Show that a frame exists in which the field is purely magnetic, and find its speed.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Start with the invariants. \( \vec{E}\cdot\vec{B} = 0 \) here, so it's zero in every frame: the fields stay perpendicular. \( E^2 - c^2B^2 < 0 \) here, so it's negative in every frame: \( E < cB \) for everyone. So no frame can have \( \vec{B} = \vec{0} \), but a frame with \( \vec{E} = \vec{0} \) is still possible.

    Choose axes with \( \vec{E} = E\vu{y} \) and \( \vec{B} = B\vu{z} \), and boost along \( x \). The transformation gives

    \[\bar{E}_y = \gamma\left( E - vB \right)\]

    which vanishes when

    \[v = \frac{E}{B}\]

    This is less than \( c \) exactly because \( E < cB \), so a real observer can move at this speed. In that frame,

    \[\bar{B}_z = \gamma\left( B - \frac{v}{c^2}E \right) = \gamma B\left( 1 - \frac{E^2}{B^2c^2} \right)\]

    which isn't zero. So that observer sees a purely magnetic field.

    If instead \( E > cB \), this would need \( v > c \), which is impossible. But then moving at \( v = c^2B/E \) would remove the magnetic field instead. The invariants decide which is possible, and every observer agrees.
