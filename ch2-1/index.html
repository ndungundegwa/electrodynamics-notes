    2.1 The Electric Field#

    The basic question#

    Electrodynamics asks one question: given some charges (the source charges), what force do they exert on another charge \( Q \) (the test charge)? The answer is given using fields. The source charges don't reach out and push \( Q \) directly. Instead they set up an electric field filling all of space, and \( Q \) feels whatever field there is at its own position.

    Coulomb's law#

    The force on \( Q \) from a single point charge \( q \), both at rest, is

    \[\vec{F} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{qQ}{\gr^2}\vu{\gr}\]

    where \( \gr \) is the separation vector from \( q \) to \( Q \), and

    \[\epsilon_0 = 8.85 \times 10^{-12}\ \frac{\text{C}^2}{\text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2}\]

    is the permittivity of free space. Like charges repel, opposite charges attract, and the force acts along the line joining them. It gets weaker as \( 1/\gr^2 \): twice as far away, a quarter of the force.

    Superposition#

    With several source charges, work out the force from each one on its own and add them as vectors. One charge being there doesn't change the force from another. This is called the principle of superposition. It isn't obviously true (many forces in nature don't work this way), but it holds exactly in classical electromagnetism, and it is what makes the subject solvable.

    The electric field#

    Divide out the test charge:

    \[\vec{E}(\vec{r}) \equiv \frac{\vec{F}}{Q} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \frac{q_i}{\gr_i^2}\vu{\gr}_i\]

    so \( \vec{F} = Q\vec{E} \). The field depends only on position, and exists at every point whether or not a test charge is there. It is the force per unit charge: put a charge \( Q \) at that point and it feels \( Q\vec{E} \). The units are newtons per coulomb, which is the same as volts per metre.

    Is the field “real”, or just a handy way of doing the sums? Electrostatics can't tell. Later chapters settle it: fields carry energy and momentum (Chapter 8), and radiation carries them away from their source for good (Chapter 11).

    Continuous distributions#

    When charge is spread out smoothly, the sum becomes an integral. There are three cases:

    DistributionDensitySmall piece of charge
    line (e.g. a wire)\( \lambda \) (C/m)\( \dd{q} = \lambda \dd{l'} \)
    surface (e.g. a sheet)\( \sigma \) (C/m²)\( \dd{q} = \sigma \dd{a'} \)
    volume (e.g. a ball)\( \rho \) (C/m³)\( \dd{q} = \rho \dd{\tau'} \)

    The volume version (the others are special cases of it) is

    \[\vec{E}(\vec{r}) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int \frac{\rho(\vec{r'})}{\gr^2}\vu{\gr}\, \dd{\tau'}\]

    \( \vu{\gr} \) points a different way for each piece of charge, so it can't come outside the integral. In practice, work out the \( x \), \( y \) and \( z \) components separately, and use symmetry to spot which components cancel.

    Example#

    Find the electric field a distance \( z \) above the midpoint of a straight line segment of length \( 2L \) carrying uniform line charge \( \lambda \).

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Lay the segment along the \( x \)-axis from \( -L \) to \( +L \). The field point is straight above the middle, at height \( z \). A small piece \( \dd{x} \) at position \( x \) has charge \( \lambda \dd{x} \) and is a distance \( \gr = \sqrt{x^2 + z^2} \) away.

    For every piece at \( +x \) there is a matching piece at \( -x \). Their sideways pushes cancel, so only the upward parts are left. Each upward part is the full contribution times \( \cos\theta = z/\sqrt{x^2 + z^2} \):

    \[E_z = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_{-L}^{L} \frac{\lambda \dd{x}}{x^2 + z^2}\frac{z}{\sqrt{x^2+z^2}} = \frac{\lambda z}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\int_{-L}^{L}\frac{\dd{x}}{(x^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\]

    The integral is a standard one:

    \[\int_{-L}^{L}\frac{\dd{x}}{(x^2+z^2)^{3/2}} = \left[ \frac{x}{z^2\sqrt{x^2+z^2}} \right]_{-L}^{L} = \frac{2L}{z^2\sqrt{L^2+z^2}}\]

    so

    \[\vec{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{2\lambda L}{z\sqrt{z^2+L^2}}\vu{z}\]

    Check the limits. Far away (\( z \gg L \)), the segment should look like a point charge \( Q = 2\lambda L \), and indeed the formula becomes \( Q/4\pi\epsilon_0 z^2 \). For an infinitely long wire (\( L \to \infty \)), it becomes \( \lambda/2\pi\epsilon_0 z \), which falls off as \( 1/z \), more slowly than a point charge's \( 1/z^2 \).

    Example#

    Find the electric field a distance \( z \) above the centre of a circular loop of radius \( R \) carrying uniform line charge \( \lambda \).

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Every piece of the loop is the same distance \( \gr = \sqrt{R^2 + z^2} \) from the field point, which makes this easy.

    By symmetry the sideways parts cancel: each piece has a partner on the opposite side of the loop pushing the other way. The upward part of each contribution has a factor \( \cos\theta = z/\sqrt{R^2+z^2} \). Both \( \gr \) and \( \cos\theta \) are the same all round the loop, so they come outside the integral, which then just adds up the charge:

    \[E_z = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{\cos\theta}{\gr^2}\int \lambda \dd{l'} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{z}{(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\left( \lambda \, 2\pi R \right)\]

    With total charge \( Q = 2\pi R\lambda \),

    \[\vec{E} = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{Qz}{(R^2+z^2)^{3/2}}\vu{z}\]

    Check. At the centre (\( z = 0 \)) the field is zero, because every push is cancelled by an opposite one. Far away (\( z \gg R \)) it becomes \( Q/4\pi\epsilon_0 z^2 \), the field of a point charge, as it should.

    Full derivation: §2.1.
