    3.2 The Method of Images#

    The idea#

    The uniqueness theorem (§3.1) says any method of finding a solution is fine. If your potential satisfies Poisson's equation in the region you care about, and matches the boundary conditions, it is the right answer.

    The method of images uses this. You remove the awkward conductor and replace it with a pretend charge, the image charge, placed where it gives the right boundary condition. The image isn't real. It is a trick that produces the correct potential in the region you care about. It must sit outside that region, so it doesn't change the charge inside.

    The classic problem#

    A point charge \( q \) is a distance \( d \) above a large, flat, grounded (\( V = 0 \)) metal plate lying in the \( xy \) plane. We don't know how the induced charge spreads over the plate, so we can't just integrate.

    Instead, forget the plate. Imagine two charges in empty space: \( +q \) at \( z = d \) and \( -q \) at \( z = -d \), like a mirror image. Every point on the plane \( z = 0 \) is equally far from both, so \( V = 0 \) there, just as for the grounded plate. And above the plane the only charge is the real \( +q \). Both conditions of the uniqueness theorem are met, so for \( z > 0 \)

    \[V(x,y,z) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[ \frac{q}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2+(z-d)^2}} - \frac{q}{\sqrt{x^2+y^2+(z+d)^2}} \right]\]

    This only holds above the plate. Below it (inside the metal) the field is really zero.

    Induced surface charge#

    Differentiate and use \( \sigma = -\epsilon_0 \pdv{V}{n} \) at the surface:

    \[\sigma(x,y) = \frac{-qd}{2\pi (x^2+y^2+d^2)^{3/2}}\]

    The induced charge is negative and bunched up right under \( q \). Adding it all up gives exactly \( -q \): every field line leaving \( q \) ends on the plate.

    Force and energy#

    The force on \( q \) is the pull from its image:

    \[\vec{F} = -\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q^2}{(2d)^2}\vu{z}\]

    So the charge is attracted towards the plate.

    But be careful: the energy is not that of the two-charge system. The pretend two-charge setup has field below the plane too; the real setup doesn't. Working out the energy directly from the force gives

    \[W = -\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q^2}{4d}\]

    which is exactly half the two-charge answer. Rule: you can get forces from the image picture, but not energies.

    The grounded sphere#

    A point charge \( q \) is a distance \( a \) from the centre of a grounded metal sphere of radius \( R \) (with \( a > R \)). The image is

    \[q' = -\frac{R}{a}q \qquad \text{placed at} \qquad b = \frac{R^2}{a}\]

    from the centre, on the line towards \( q \). The image is smaller than \( q \) and sits inside the sphere, which is outside the region we care about. The total induced charge on the sphere is \( q' \). If instead the sphere is isolated and uncharged (not grounded), add a second image \( -q' \) at the centre to keep its total charge zero.

    Example#

    Verify that the image construction for the grounded sphere places the surface at zero potential.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Put the sphere's centre at the origin, \( q \) on the \( z \)-axis at distance \( a \), and the image \( q' = -Rq/a \) at \( b = R^2/a \).

    By the cosine rule, a point on the sphere at angle \( \theta \) is these distances from the two charges:

    \[\gr = \sqrt{R^2 + a^2 - 2Ra\cos\theta}, \qquad \gr' = \sqrt{R^2 + b^2 - 2Rb\cos\theta}\]

    Put \( b = R^2/a \) into the second and take out a factor \( R/a \):

    \[\gr' = \sqrt{R^2 + \frac{R^4}{a^2} - \frac{2R^3\cos\theta}{a}} = \frac{R}{a}\sqrt{a^2 + R^2 - 2Ra\cos\theta} = \frac{R}{a}\gr\]

    So at every point on the sphere, the distance to the image is \( R/a \) times the distance to \( q \). The potential is

    \[V = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left( \frac{q}{\gr} + \frac{q'}{\gr'} \right) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left( \frac{q}{\gr} + \frac{-Rq/a}{(R/a)\gr} \right) = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left( \frac{q}{\gr} - \frac{q}{\gr} \right) = 0\]

    The two terms cancel exactly, for every \( \theta \), so the whole sphere is at zero potential. ✓ The values of \( q' \) and \( b \) are exactly the ones that keep the ratio \( \gr'/\gr \) the same all over the sphere.

    Example#

    A point charge \( q \) is held at distance \( d \) above an infinite grounded plane. Find the work required to remove it to infinity, and reconcile the result with the energy of the equivalent two-charge system.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    At height \( z \), the image (at \( -z \)) pulls the charge down with force \( q^2/4\pi\epsilon_0 (2z)^2 \). The work needed to pull it from \( d \) out to infinity is

    \[W = \int_d^\infty \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q^2}{4z^2}\dd{z} = \frac{q^2}{16\pi\epsilon_0}\left[ -\frac{1}{z} \right]_d^\infty = \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q^2}{4d}\]

    So the energy of the setup is \( W = -q^2/16\pi\epsilon_0 d \).

    Two real charges \( +q \) and \( -q \) a distance \( 2d \) apart would have energy \( -q^2/8\pi\epsilon_0 d \): twice as much.

    Why the difference? The energy is stored in the field. With two real charges, the field fills all of space. With the real plate, the field fills only the half above it; below, it's zero. By symmetry that's exactly half the field energy, hence the factor of two.

    Another way to see it: with two real charges, pulling them apart moves both charges, and both movements cost work. With the plate, only the real charge is moved by you; the image just follows along for free.

    Full derivation: §3.2.
