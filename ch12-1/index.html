    12.1 The Special Theory of Relativity#

    Einstein's postulates#

    In ordinary mechanics, the laws are the same in every inertial frame (any frame moving at constant velocity): drop a ball on a smoothly moving train and it falls just as it does on the platform. Electrodynamics seemed to break this rule. Maxwell's equations predict one particular speed, \( c \), and people assumed that meant speed relative to some medium, the “ether”. But experiments could find no ether. Einstein's answer was to take both ideas as basic starting points:

    1. The principle of relativity. The laws of physics are the same in all inertial frames.
    2. The speed of light is universal. Light in vacuum travels at the same speed \( c \) for every inertial observer, however fast the source is moving.

    If Maxwell's equations count as laws of physics, the second actually follows from the first. What has to go is the everyday rule for adding velocities, and with it the idea that time is the same for everyone.

    Three consequences#

    Simultaneity is relative. Two events that happen at the same time in one frame happen at different times in a frame moving relative to it. There's no universal “now”.

    Moving clocks run slow (time dilation):

    \[\Delta t = \gamma \Delta \bar{t}, \qquad \gamma \equiv \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - v^2/c^2}}\]

    \( \Delta\bar{t} \) is the proper time: the time measured by a clock that is present at both events (so it sees them happen at the same place). Everyone else measures a longer time. \( \gamma \) is always at least 1, and is only noticeably bigger than 1 near light speed.

    Moving objects are shorter (Lorentz contraction) along their direction of motion:

    \[\Delta x = \frac{\Delta\bar{x}}{\gamma}\]

    Sizes at right angles to the motion don't change.

    Both effects work both ways: each observer finds the other's clocks slow and rulers short. That isn't a contradiction, because they disagree about which events are simultaneous, and comparing clocks or rulers depends on that.

    The Lorentz transformations#

    For a frame moving at speed \( v \) along the shared \( x \)-axis:

    \[\bar{x} = \gamma(x - vt), \qquad \bar{y} = y, \qquad \bar{z} = z, \qquad \bar{t} = \gamma\left( t - \frac{v}{c^2}x \right)\]

    When \( v \ll c \), \( \gamma \approx 1 \) and these become the everyday (Galilean) rules. So ordinary mechanics is still right at everyday speeds.

    The rule for adding velocities becomes

    \[\bar{u} = \frac{u - v}{1 - uv/c^2}\]

    This never gives a speed above \( c \) if neither input is above \( c \). Put in \( u = c \) and you get \( \bar{u} = c \): light has the same speed for everyone, as it should.

    Spacetime#

    The interval between two events,

    \[I \equiv -\left( c\Delta t \right)^2 + \left( \Delta x \right)^2 + \left( \Delta y \right)^2 + \left( \Delta z \right)^2\]

    is the same in every inertial frame, even though the time and distance separately aren't. (It's like how rotating your axes changes \( \Delta x \) and \( \Delta y \) but not the distance.) Its sign sorts pairs of events into three kinds:

    SignNameMeaning
    \( I < 0 \)timelikesome observer sees both at the same place; one could cause the other
    \( I > 0 \)spacelikesome observer sees them at the same time; neither can cause the other
    \( I = 0 \)lightlikea light signal could connect them exactly

    Everyone agrees which kind a pair is. That's what saves cause and effect: different observers can disagree about the order of two events only if they are spacelike, and then neither could have caused the other anyway.

    Quantities that transform like \( (ct, x, y, z) \) are called four-vectors. For any two of them, \( -a^0b^0 + a^1b^1 + a^2b^2 + a^3b^3 \) is the same in every frame.

    Example#

    Muons created in the upper atmosphere have a proper lifetime of \( 2.2\ \mu\text{s} \) and travel at \( 0.998c \). Show that they should not reach the ground classically, and resolve the discrepancy.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Without relativity. In \( 2.2\ \mu\text{s} \) at \( 0.998c \), a muon travels

    \[d = (0.998)(3\times10^8)(2.2\times10^{-6}) \approx 660\ \text{m}\]

    Muons are made about 10 km up, so hardly any should reach the ground. But detectors at sea level find lots of them.

    With relativity. The Lorentz factor is

    \[\gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - (0.998)^2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{0.004}} \approx 15.8\]

    Seen from the ground, the muon's internal clock runs slow, so it lives longer:

    \[\Delta t = \gamma\Delta\bar{t} = 15.8(2.2\times10^{-6}) = 3.5\times10^{-5}\ \text{s}\]

    In that time it travels about \( 10.4 \) km, enough to reach the ground. ✓

    Seen by the muon, the story is different but gives the same result. Its lifetime is the normal \( 2.2\ \mu\text{s} \). What changes is the distance: the atmosphere rushing past at \( 0.998c \) is Lorentz-contracted to

    \[\frac{10\ \text{km}}{15.8} \approx 630\ \text{m}\]

    which it covers easily. The two observers agree that the muon arrives, but disagree completely about why.

    Example#

    A spaceship travelling at \( 0.8c \) fires a projectile forward at \( 0.8c \) relative to itself. Find the projectile's speed in the ground frame.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Everyday addition would give \( 1.6c \), faster than light. Using the relativistic rule, with \( u' = 0.8c \) (projectile relative to ship) and \( v = 0.8c \) (ship):

    \[u = \frac{u' + v}{1 + u'v/c^2} = \frac{0.8c + 0.8c}{1 + (0.8)(0.8)} = \frac{1.6c}{1.64} \approx 0.976c\]

    Less than \( c \), as it must be.

    The bottom line of the fraction is what keeps it below \( c \). For everyday speeds \( u'v/c^2 \) is incredibly tiny (for two jets it's about \( 10^{-12} \)), so the normal rule works perfectly well. That's why nobody noticed for centuries.

    Check with light. If the ship shines a torch forward instead, \( u' = c \), and

    \[u = \frac{c + v}{1 + v/c} = \frac{c(c+v)}{c+v} = c\]

    whatever \( v \) is. The light still travels at exactly \( c \) for the ground observer, just as the second postulate says.
