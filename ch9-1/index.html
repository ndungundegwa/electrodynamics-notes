    9.1 Waves in One Dimension#

    The wave equation#

    A wave is a disturbance that travels along while keeping its shape (think of a pulse sent down a rope). If a shape \( f(z,t) \) moves in the \( +z \) direction at speed \( v \) without changing, then at time \( t \) it's just the starting shape slid along by \( vt \):

    \[f(z,t) = g(z - vt)\]

    Any function of \( z - vt \) is a wave moving right; any function of \( z + vt \) is a wave moving left. Differentiate twice and both satisfy

    \[\pdv{^2 f}{z^2} = \frac{1}{v^2}\pdv{^2 f}{t^2}\]

    the wave equation. It's linear, so you can add solutions together. Its general solution is a right-moving wave plus a left-moving wave.

    This is worth remembering: whenever a law of physics leads to this equation, there are waves, and you can read off their speed from the number in front of the time derivative.

    Sinusoidal waves#

    The most important solution is

    \[f(z,t) = A\cos\left( kz - \omega t + \delta \right)\]

    with amplitude \( A \) (height), wave number \( k \), angular frequency \( \omega \), and phase constant \( \delta \) (where in the cycle it starts). These are connected to the wavelength \( \lambda \), period \( T \) and speed:

    \[\lambda = \frac{2\pi}{k}, \qquad T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega}, \qquad v = \frac{\omega}{k}\]

    Sine waves matter for more than being simple. By Fourier analysis, any wave shape can be built by adding sine waves. So if you can handle the sine wave, you can handle everything.

    Complex notation#

    Working with sines and cosines is tedious, so we write waves as

    \[\tilde{f}(z,t) = \tilde{A}e^{i(kz-\omega t)}, \qquad \tilde{A} = Ae^{i\delta}\]

    and agree that the real, physical wave is the real part. The complex amplitude \( \tilde{A} \) packs the height and the starting phase into one number.

    Exponentials are much easier to differentiate and multiply than sines and cosines, and that's the only reason for doing this. It works because the wave equation is linear, so real and imaginary parts never mix. It fails for anything non-linear, such as squaring to get an intensity. Take the real part first in those cases.

    Reflection and transmission#

    Where two different media join (say, a light rope tied to a heavy one), a wave is partly reflected and partly passed on. Requiring the rope to stay joined (the wave is continuous) and not kinked (its slope is continuous) at the knot gives:

    \[\tilde{A}_R = \left( \frac{v_2 - v_1}{v_2 + v_1} \right)\tilde{A}_I, \qquad \tilde{A}_T = \left( \frac{2v_2}{v_2+v_1} \right)\tilde{A}_I\]

    (I = incident, R = reflected, T = transmitted.) The frequency is the same on both sides, because the knot can only wiggle at one rate. The speed and wavelength change.

    Watch the sign. If \( v_2 < v_1 \) (the second medium is “heavier”), the reflected wave comes back upside down. In the extreme \( v_2 \to 0 \) (the end is tied to a wall), all of the wave reflects, upside down.

    Polarization#

    A wave is transverse if the wiggle is at right angles to the direction of travel (a shaken rope), and longitudinal if it's along it (sound). Transverse waves have an extra choice: which way, within the perpendicular plane, the wiggle points. That direction is the polarization \( \vu{n} \).

    Any polarization can be split into two independent directions, so a transverse wave has two possible polarizations; a longitudinal wave has one. Electromagnetic waves are transverse (§9.2). That's why polarizing sunglasses work.

    Example#

    A wave travels along a string of mass density \( \mu_1 \) and meets a knot joining it to a heavier string of density \( \mu_2 = 4\mu_1 \), both under the same tension \( T \). Find the reflected and transmitted amplitudes.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    The wave speed on a string is \( v = \sqrt{T/\mu} \). With the same tension,

    \[\frac{v_2}{v_1} = \sqrt{\frac{\mu_1}{\mu_2}} = \sqrt{\frac{1}{4}} = \frac{1}{2}\]

    so \( v_2 = v_1/2 \). Put this into the formulas:

    \[\tilde{A}_R = \left( \frac{v_1/2 - v_1}{v_1/2 + v_1} \right)\tilde{A}_I = \left( \frac{-1/2}{3/2} \right)\tilde{A}_I = -\frac{1}{3}\tilde{A}_I\] \[\tilde{A}_T = \left( \frac{2(v_1/2)}{v_1/2 + v_1} \right)\tilde{A}_I = \frac{2}{3}\tilde{A}_I\]

    The reflected wave is a third as tall and upside down (the minus sign, which is the same as a phase shift of \( \pi \)). That always happens when reflecting off a heavier, slower medium.

    The transmitted wave is upright and two-thirds as tall.

    Check: at the knot, the string on the left (incident + reflected) must be at the same height as the string on the right (transmitted): \( 1 - \frac{1}{3} = \frac{2}{3} \). ✓ Note that amplitudes don't have to “add up” in any other sense. It's energy that must balance, and the energy carried also depends on the string's density and wave speed, not just the amplitude.

    Example#

    Show that \( f(z,t) = A e^{-b(z-vt)^2} \) satisfies the wave equation, and describe the motion.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    The function only depends on \( z \) and \( t \) through the combination \( u \equiv z - vt \). By the chain rule, \( \pdv{}{z} = \dv{}{u} \) and \( \pdv{}{t} = -v\dv{}{u} \). Doing each twice,

    \[\pdv{^2 f}{z^2} = \dv{^2 f}{u^2}, \qquad \pdv{^2 f}{t^2} = v^2 \dv{^2 f}{u^2}\]

    so

    \[\pdv{^2 f}{z^2} = \frac{1}{v^2}\pdv{^2 f}{t^2}\]

    as required. ✓ We never used the actual shape of the function: any smooth function of \( z - vt \) satisfies the wave equation.

    The motion: a bell-shaped (Gaussian) bump of height \( A \) and width about \( 1/\sqrt{b} \), sliding in the \( +z \) direction at speed \( v \) without changing shape. Keeping its shape means the medium is non-dispersive: all frequencies travel at the same speed. When that isn't true (e.g. waves in conductors, §9.4), a pulse spreads out as it goes.

    Full derivation: §9.1.
