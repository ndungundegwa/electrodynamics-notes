    11.3 The Radiation Reaction#

    The recoil#

    An accelerating charge radiates energy, and that energy has to come from somewhere. So a charged particle must respond to a push slightly differently from an uncharged particle of the same mass: its own fields push back on it. This push-back is the radiation reaction. (Think of the kick a gun gives when it fires.)

    The Abraham-Lorentz formula#

    A first guess: set the work done by the reaction force equal to the radiated power. But that's not true moment by moment. The radiated power was worked out on a sphere at infinity, where the velocity field doesn't reach. Near the particle, though, the velocity field does hold energy, and that energy swaps back and forth between particle and field, separately from the radiation that escapes.

    The fix is to average over a stretch of time that starts and ends with the particle in the same state, so the velocity-field energy is the same at both ends:

    \[\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\vec{F}_{\text{rad}}\cdot\vec{v}\dd{t} = -\frac{\mu_0 q^2}{6\pi c}\int_{t_1}^{t_2}a^2\dd{t}\]

    Integrate the right side by parts. The boundary term vanishes (same state at both ends), and the equation is satisfied by

    \[\vec{F}_{\text{rad}} = \frac{\mu_0 q^2}{6\pi c}\dot{\vec{a}}\]

    the Abraham-Lorentz formula. Strange feature: the force depends on how fast the acceleration is changing. So it can be non-zero when the particle isn't radiating, and zero when it is (e.g. constant acceleration). “Radiation reaction” is a misleading name; “field reaction” would be better.

    This argument proves less than it seems. It only pins down the average of the force along the direction of motion, over special time intervals. It gives the simplest formula consistent with energy conservation, not the only possible one.

    Two problems#

    Runaway motion. With no outside force, Newton's second law becomes \( \mu_0q^2\dot{a}/6\pi c = ma \), whose solution is

    \[a(t) = a_0 e^{t/\tau}, \qquad \tau \equiv \frac{\mu_0 q^2}{6\pi m c}\]

    For an electron \( \tau \approx 6\times10^{-24} \) s. So a particle left completely alone could spontaneously accelerate faster and faster forever, out of nothing. Obviously absurd.

    Pre-acceleration. You can rule out runaways by insisting \( a_0 = 0 \), but then something worse happens: the particle starts to move before the force is applied, by a time of about \( \tau \). Effect before cause.

    Neither problem has been fully solved, more than a century later, and both are still there in the relativistic version.

    The self-force#

    What causes the reaction? The charge's own fields pushing on the charge itself. For a true point charge the fields are infinite at the particle, so instead we calculate for a small spread-out charge and then shrink it to zero size.

    The key fact: when charges move, the force of one piece of the charge on another is not equal and opposite to the force back (Newton's third law fails for moving charges, §8.2). Add up all these mismatches over every pair of pieces, and there's a net force of the charge on itself.

    Take a simple “dumbbell”: total charge \( q \) split into two halves a distance \( d \) apart. Expanding for small \( d \) leaves two important terms:

    \[\vec{F}_{\text{self}} = \frac{q^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\left[ -\frac{a}{4c^2 d} + \frac{\dot{a}}{3c^3} + O(d) \right]\vu{x}\]

    The first term is proportional to the acceleration. Move it to the other side of \( \vec{F} = m\vec{a} \) and it just adds to the mass. That makes sense: the electrostatic energy of the charge counts as mass through \( E = mc^2 \). Trouble is, it becomes infinite as \( d\to 0 \). The standard fix is to assume the particle's “bare” mass has a matching infinity the other way, so the measured mass is finite. This trick is called mass renormalization, and it's used in quantum electrodynamics too.

    The second term is the radiation reaction. Once you also include each end's force on itself, it matches the Abraham-Lorentz formula exactly. That confirms the formula independently of the energy argument above.

    Example#

    A charge is attached to a spring of natural frequency \( \omega_0 \) and oscillates at frequency \( \omega \). Find the damping constant produced by the radiation reaction.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    The equation of motion includes the spring, the radiation reaction, and whatever drives the oscillation:

    \[m\ddot{x} = -m\omega_0^2 x + m\tau\dddot{x} + F_{\text{driving}}\]

    For oscillation at frequency \( \omega \), \( x = x_0\cos(\omega t + \delta) \). Differentiating twice just multiplies by \( -\omega^2 \), so

    \[\dddot{x} = -\omega^2\dot{x}\]

    That turns the strange third-derivative term into an ordinary friction term proportional to velocity:

    \[m\ddot{x} + m\gamma\dot{x} + m\omega_0^2 x = F_{\text{driving}}, \qquad \gamma = \omega^2\tau\]

    This is the standard damped, driven oscillator, with damping constant \( \gamma = \omega^2\tau \).

    In §9.4 we simply assumed a friction force proportional to velocity when modelling dispersion. Now we see where (part of) it comes from: radiation damping. For sinusoidal motion it looks exactly like velocity-proportional friction, and now we even know its size.

    Example#

    Estimate \( \tau \) for an electron and the distance light travels in that time. Comment on when the radiation reaction matters.

    Solution triple-click to reveal
    \[\tau = \frac{\mu_0 q^2}{6\pi m c} = \frac{(1.26\times10^{-6})(1.6\times10^{-19})^2}{6\pi(9.11\times10^{-31})(3.00\times10^8)}\]

    The top is \( 3.22\times10^{-44} \) and the bottom \( 5.15\times10^{-21} \), so

    \[\tau \approx 6\times10^{-24}\ \text{s}\]

    In that time light travels

    \[c\tau \approx (3\times10^8)(6\times10^{-24}) \approx 2\times10^{-15}\ \text{m}\]

    That's about the “classical electron radius”, roughly the size of an atomic nucleus, and about 50,000 times smaller than an atom.

    So the radiation reaction only matters if the motion changes noticeably within about \( 10^{-23} \) s, or over distances of about a femtometre. In almost every classical problem it is a tiny correction, which is why we could ignore it in Chapters 1–10.

    It also explains why physicists live with the strange problems above. Pre-acceleration happens only about \( 10^{-23} \) s early, far too short to measure, and at scales where classical physics isn't expected to work anyway. That territory belongs to quantum electrodynamics.
