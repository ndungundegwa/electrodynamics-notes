    8.2 Momentum#

    Newton's third law fails#

    Picture two charges, one moving along the \( x \)-axis and the other along the \( y \)-axis, both heading towards the origin. The electric forces between them are equal and opposite, as Newton's third law expects. The magnetic forces are not. Each charge's magnetic field pushes on the other, but the two pushes point in directions that don't cancel.

    So Newton's third law fails for moving charges. Conservation of momentum is usually proved from the third law, so it looks as if momentum conservation fails too.

    It doesn't. The missing momentum is carried by the fields.

    The Maxwell stress tensor#

    The total electromagnetic force on all the charges inside a volume can be written using only the fields on the surface around it:

    \[\vec{F} = \oint_S \overleftrightarrow{T}\cdot\dd{\vec{a}} - \epsilon_0\mu_0\dv{}{t}\int_V \vec{S}\dd{\tau}\]

    where the Maxwell stress tensor is

    \[T_{ij} \equiv \epsilon_0\left( E_i E_j - \frac{1}{2}\delta_{ij}E^2 \right) + \frac{1}{\mu_0}\left( B_i B_j - \frac{1}{2}\delta_{ij}B^2 \right)\]

    It has two indices because a force on a surface depends on two directions: \( T_{ij} \) is the force per unit area in direction \( i \) on a surface facing direction \( j \). The diagonal entries (\( i = j \)) are pushes or pulls straight through the surface (pressure or tension). The others are sideways (shear) forces.

    A helpful picture (due to Faraday): field lines act like stretched rubber bands that also push sideways on their neighbours. They pull along their length and push apart across it.

    What it's good for: you can find the force on a group of charges by integrating over any surface around them, without knowing what's going on inside.

    Conservation of momentum#

    The fields carry momentum, with density

    \[\vec{g} = \epsilon_0\mu_0 \vec{S} = \epsilon_0\left( \vec{E}\cross\vec{B} \right)\]

    That's just the Poynting vector divided by \( c^2 \). Wherever energy flows, momentum flows with it.

    Conservation of momentum then has the usual shape:

    \[\pdv{}{t}\left( \vec{p}_{\text{mech}} + \vec{p}_{\text{em}} \right) = \oint_S \overleftrightarrow{T}\cdot\dd{\vec{a}}\]

    The total momentum (of the charges plus the fields) only changes by momentum flowing in or out through the surface. The stress tensor describes that flow.

    So momentum conservation survives, as long as you count the momentum in the fields. This is the strongest reason to believe fields are real and not just a calculating device: they carry energy and momentum, and you need both to make the books balance.

    An immediate consequence is radiation pressure. Light hitting a surface hands over momentum, so it pushes on it with pressure

    \[P = \frac{I}{c}\]

    if the light is absorbed, and twice that if it's perfectly reflected.

    Angular momentum#

    Since the fields carry momentum \( \vec{g} \), they also carry angular momentum, with density

    \[\vec{\ell} = \vec{r}\cross\vec{g} = \epsilon_0\left[ \vec{r}\cross\left( \vec{E}\cross\vec{B} \right) \right]\]

    Remarkably, fields that aren't changing at all can store angular momentum. An arrangement of electric and magnetic fields sitting perfectly still can hold angular momentum, which gets handed to the charges (making something start to spin) when the fields are switched off. This is the “Feynman disc paradox”.

    Example#

    Sunlight strikes the Earth with intensity about \( 1300\ \text{W/m}^2 \). Find the radiation pressure on a perfectly absorbing surface, and compare with atmospheric pressure.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    For complete absorption, the momentum delivered per second per square metre is the energy flow divided by \( c \):

    \[P = \frac{I}{c} = \frac{1300}{3.00\times10^8} \approx 4.3\times10^{-6}\ \text{Pa}\]

    Atmospheric pressure is about \( 1.0\times10^5 \) Pa, so sunlight pushes about \( 2\times10^{10} \) times more weakly.

    That's why you never feel it. But where nothing else competes, it matters: it pushes comet tails away from the Sun, helps hold up the insides of stars, and drives solar sails, where a tiny push kept up for months gives a spacecraft real speed.

    For a perfect mirror the push doubles, because the light bounces back instead of just stopping: \( P = 2I/c \). (Like a ball bouncing off a wall versus sticking to it.)

    Example#

    Two point charges \( +q \) and \( -q \) are held a distance \( d \) apart. Use the stress tensor to find the force on the positive charge, integrating over the plane midway between them.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Put \( +q \) at \( z = +d/2 \) and \( -q \) at \( z = -d/2 \). To find the force on \( +q \), use the region \( z > 0 \). Its boundary is the plane \( z = 0 \) (plus a surface at infinity, where the fields vanish). The outward normal from this region points down, \( -\vu{z} \), so only \( T_{zz} \) is needed, and there's no magnetic field:

    \[F_z = -\int T_{zz}\, \dd{a}, \qquad T_{zz} = \frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\left( E_z^2 - E_x^2 - E_y^2 \right)\]

    The field on the midplane. At a point on the plane, the field from \( +q \) points away from \( +q \) (sideways and downward). The field from \( -q \) points towards \( -q \) (sideways back in, and downward). The sideways parts cancel and the downward parts add. So on the midplane the field points straight down, along \( -\vu{z} \): \( E_x = E_y = 0 \).

    At distance \( s \) from the axis, each charge is \( \gr = \sqrt{s^2 + d^2/4} \) away, and each contributes a downward part with factor \( (d/2)/\gr \):

    \[E_z = -2\left( \frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q}{\gr^2} \right)\frac{d/2}{\gr} = -\frac{q d}{4\pi\epsilon_0 (s^2+d^2/4)^{3/2}}\]

    So \( T_{zz} = \frac{\epsilon_0}{2}E_z^2 \), which is positive: the field lines crossing the plane are “stretched rubber bands” pulling the two halves together.

    Integrate over the plane, with \( \dd{a} = 2\pi s \dd{s} \):

    \[F_z = -\frac{\epsilon_0}{2}\int_0^\infty \left( \frac{qd}{4\pi\epsilon_0(s^2+d^2/4)^{3/2}} \right)^2 2\pi s\dd{s} = -\frac{q^2 d^2}{16\pi\epsilon_0}\int_0^\infty \frac{s \dd{s}}{(s^2+d^2/4)^3}\]

    Substituting \( u = s^2 + d^2/4 \), the integral is \( 1/[4(d^2/4)^2] = 4/d^4 \), so

    \[F_z = -\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\frac{q^2}{d^2}\]

    That's Coulomb's law, with the minus sign meaning \( +q \) is pulled down towards \( -q \): attraction. ✓ We never needed the field at either charge, only on a surface between them. That's what the stress tensor is good for.

    Full derivation: §8.2.
