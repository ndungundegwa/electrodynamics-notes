    9.3 Electromagnetic Waves in Matter#

    Waves in linear materials#

    Inside a linear material with no free charge or current, Maxwell's equations look just like the vacuum ones with \( \epsilon_0 \to \epsilon \) and \( \mu_0 \to \mu \). So everything in §9.2 carries over with that swap, and waves travel at

    \[v = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\epsilon\mu}} = \frac{c}{n}\]

    where the index of refraction is

    \[n \equiv \sqrt{\frac{\epsilon\mu}{\epsilon_0\mu_0}} \approx \sqrt{\epsilon_r}\]

    The approximation works because most transparent materials are barely magnetic, \( \mu \approx \mu_0 \).

    For ordinary materials \( n > 1 \), so light is slower in a material than in vacuum (in glass, about \( c/1.5 \)). The energy density and intensity become

    \[u = \epsilon E^2, \qquad I = \frac{1}{2}\epsilon v E_0^2\]

    Reflection and transmission, straight on#

    When a wave hits a flat boundary between two materials head-on, part of it reflects and part goes through. Using the boundary conditions of §7.3 (the parallel parts of \( \vec{E} \) and \( \vec{H} \) don't jump) gives formulas just like those for waves on a string (§9.1):

    \[\tilde{E}_{0_R} = \left( \frac{n_1 - n_2}{n_1 + n_2} \right)\tilde{E}_{0_I}, \qquad \tilde{E}_{0_T} = \left( \frac{2n_1}{n_1+n_2} \right)\tilde{E}_{0_I}\]

    If \( n_2 > n_1 \) the reflected amplitude is negative: the wave comes back upside down when it reflects off a “denser” material, just like reflection off a heavier string.

    The fractions of energy reflected (\( R \)) and transmitted (\( T \)) are

    \[R = \left( \frac{n_1-n_2}{n_1+n_2} \right)^2, \qquad T = \frac{4n_1n_2}{(n_1+n_2)^2}\]

    and \( R + T = 1 \): no energy goes missing.

    Hitting at an angle#

    When a wave hits the boundary at an angle, three laws follow just from requiring that the incoming, reflected and transmitted waves stay in step with each other all along the boundary:

    • The three rays and the normal (the line perpendicular to the surface) all lie in one plane.
    • Law of reflection: \( \theta_I = \theta_R \) (angle in = angle out).
    • Snell's law: \( n_1\sin\theta_I = n_2\sin\theta_T \). Light bends towards the normal going into a denser material.

    These only depend on the geometry of waves, not on what kind of wave it is. How much light reflects is a different question, which needs the boundary conditions and gives Fresnel's equations. For polarization in the plane of incidence,

    \[\frac{E_{0_R}}{E_{0_I}} = \frac{\alpha - \beta}{\alpha + \beta}, \qquad \alpha \equiv \frac{\cos\theta_T}{\cos\theta_I}, \quad \beta \equiv \frac{n_2}{n_1}\]

    Two important results:

    Brewster's angle. The reflected wave above disappears when \( \alpha = \beta \), at an angle with \( \tan\theta_B = n_2/n_1 \). At this angle only the other polarization reflects, so the reflected light is completely polarized. Glare off water or a road is mostly reflected light near this angle, polarized horizontally, which is why polarizing sunglasses (which block horizontal polarization) cut glare.

    Total internal reflection. Going from a denser to a less dense material (\( n_1 > n_2 \)), Snell's law has no answer beyond the critical angle

    \[\sin\theta_c = \frac{n_2}{n_1}\]

    and all the light reflects. This is how optical fibres trap light and carry internet signals under the ocean.

    Example#

    Light passes from air (\( n=1.0 \)) into glass (\( n=1.5 \)) at normal incidence. Find the fraction of energy reflected, and Brewster's angle for the interface.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Reflection. Put the numbers into \( R \):

    \[R = \left( \frac{1.0 - 1.5}{1.0 + 1.5} \right)^2 = \left( \frac{-0.5}{2.5} \right)^2 = (0.2)^2 = 0.04\]

    So 4% of the energy reflects and 96% goes into the glass. (That's the faint reflection you see in a shop window.) The amplitude ratio is negative, so the reflected wave is flipped, but you can't see that in the brightness.

    Losing 4% at every glass surface adds up in a camera lens with many elements. That's why lenses have anti-reflection coatings.

    Brewster's angle.

    \[\tan\theta_B = \frac{n_2}{n_1} = 1.5 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \theta_B = \arctan(1.5) = 56.3^\circ\]

    Light hitting the glass at \( 56.3^\circ \) from the normal reflects fully polarized, perpendicular to the plane of incidence. For flat horizontal surfaces like water and roads, that means horizontally polarized, which is exactly what polarizing sunglasses block.

    Example#

    Find the critical angle for a glass-air interface with \( n_{\text{glass}} = 1.5 \), and explain why light escapes at all from a fibre bent too sharply.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Total internal reflection only happens going from the denser material to the less dense one (glass to air). At the critical angle, the refracted ray would just skim along the surface, \( \theta_T = 90^\circ \):

    \[\sin\theta_c = \frac{n_2}{n_1} = \frac{1.0}{1.5} = 0.667 \quad \Rightarrow \quad \theta_c = 41.8^\circ\]

    Light hitting the inside surface at more than \( 41.8^\circ \) from the normal is completely reflected.

    In a straight fibre, light travels at a shallow slant and hits the walls at large angles (well past \( 41.8^\circ \)), so it bounces along forever. Bend the fibre sharply, and at the bend the light hits the outer wall more head-on. Once the angle drops below \( 41.8^\circ \), light leaks out. That's why optical fibres have a minimum bending radius.

    “Total” reflection doesn't mean the field is exactly zero outside the glass. A weak evanescent wave pokes a tiny distance out (about a wavelength), dying away quickly and carrying no energy. But bring another piece of glass that close and energy can hop across the gap: this is “frustrated total internal reflection”, a light-wave version of quantum tunnelling.

    Full derivation: §9.3.
