    5.3 The Divergence and Curl of B#

    The Helmholtz theorem says a field is fixed by its divergence and curl. Both can be worked out from the Biot-Savart law.

    The divergence#

    \[\div{\vec{B}} = 0\]

    everywhere, always, not just for steady currents. This is one of Maxwell's equations. It says there are no magnetic monopoles: no isolated north or south poles. Cut a bar magnet in half and you get two smaller magnets, each with both poles. Magnetic field lines never start or stop; they always form closed loops.

    Compare \( \div{\vec{E}} = \rho/\epsilon_0 \): electric field lines start and end on charges. There is no magnetic charge, so there's nothing on the right-hand side.

    The curl: Ampère's law#

    \[\curl{\vec{B}} = \mu_0 \vec{J}\]

    or, using Stokes' theorem, in integral form:

    \[\oint \vec{B}\cdot\dd{\vec{l}} = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}}\]

    Going round any closed loop, adding up \( \vec{B} \) along the loop gives \( \mu_0 \) times the current passing through the loop. \( I_{\text{enc}} \) is the current through any surface whose edge is the loop. It doesn't matter which surface you pick: for steady currents the same current passes through all of them.

    This form of Ampère's law only works for steady currents. If \( \div{\vec{J}} \neq 0 \) (charge piling up somewhere, as on a charging capacitor), it contradicts itself: the divergence of a curl must be zero, but \( \div{(\mu_0\vec{J})} \neq 0 \). Maxwell's fix is in §7.3.

    Using Ampère's law#

    Ampère's law always holds, but it only gives you \( \vec{B} \) easily when symmetry makes \( \vec{B} \) the same size along parts of a loop, and either along the loop or at right angles to it. That happens for:

    • long straight wires: use a circle around the wire
    • flat sheets of current: use a rectangle poking through the sheet
    • long solenoids (coils): use a rectangle with one side inside and one outside
    • toroids (doughnut-shaped coils): use a circle inside the doughnut

    Standard results:

    \[B_{\text{wire}} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi s}, \qquad B_{\text{sheet}} = \frac{\mu_0 K}{2}, \qquad B_{\text{solenoid}} = \mu_0 n I\]

    where \( n \) is the number of turns per unit length. A solenoid's field is uniform inside and zero outside. A sheet's field doesn't weaken with distance, just like the electric field of a charged plane.

    Divergence\( \div{\vec{E}} = \rho/\epsilon_0 \)\( \div{\vec{B}} = 0 \)

    Integral lawGaussAmpère
    Potentialscalar \( V \)vector \( \vec{A} \)

    The two are mirror images. \( \vec{E} \) spreads out but doesn't swirl; \( \vec{B} \) swirls but doesn't spread out. That's why \( \vec{E} \) gets a scalar potential and \( \vec{B} \) gets a vector potential (§5.4).

    Example#

    Find the magnetic field inside and outside a long solenoid of \( n \) turns per unit length carrying current \( I \).

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    First, which way does \( \vec{B} \) point? Along the axis. It can't point outward from the axis: then field lines would leave a cylinder around the axis without coming back, breaking \( \div{\vec{B}} = 0 \). And it can't circle the axis, because no current flows along the axis.

    Outside. Draw a rectangular loop with both long sides outside the solenoid, at different distances from it. No current passes through the loop, so the field along the two long sides must be equal. So the field outside is the same at all distances. Very far away it must be zero, so it is zero everywhere outside:

    \[\vec{B}_{\text{out}} = \vec{0}\]

    Inside. Draw a rectangular loop of length \( L \) with one long side inside the solenoid (along the axis) and the other outside. Only the inside edge contributes: the outside edge is in zero field, and the short sides are at right angles to \( \vec{B} \). The loop is crossed by \( nL \) turns of wire, each carrying \( I \):

    \[BL = \mu_0 n L I \quad \Rightarrow \quad B = \mu_0 n I\]

    It didn't matter how far inside we put the edge, so the field is uniform inside. The solenoid is the magnetic version of a parallel-plate capacitor: a strong, uniform field neatly trapped inside.

    Example#

    A thick wire of radius \( a \) carries a total current \( I \) distributed uniformly over its cross-section. Find the field inside and outside.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    The current density is \( J = I/\pi a^2 \), the same across the wire. By symmetry, \( \vec{B} \) circles the axis and its size depends only on the distance \( s \). Use a circular loop of radius \( s \) around the axis. On it, \( \vec{B} \) runs along the loop and has the same size everywhere:

    \[\oint \vec{B}\cdot\dd{\vec{l}} = B(s)\,2\pi s = \mu_0 I_{\text{enc}}\]

    Outside (\( s > a \)), all the current passes through the loop:

    \[B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi s}\]

    the same as a thin wire. From outside you can't tell how thick the wire is.

    Inside (\( s < a \)), only the current within radius \( s \) passes through:

    \[I_{\text{enc}} = J\pi s^2 = I\frac{s^2}{a^2}\]

    so

    \[B(s)\,2\pi s = \mu_0 I \frac{s^2}{a^2} \quad \Rightarrow \quad B = \frac{\mu_0 I s}{2\pi a^2}\]

    The field rises steadily from zero at the centre to its largest value at the surface, then falls as \( 1/s \) outside. Compare the uniformly charged ball in §2.2: same shape of graph, for the same reason. Inside, the enclosed source grows faster than the loop does; outside, it stops growing.

    Full derivation: §5.3.
