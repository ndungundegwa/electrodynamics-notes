    8.1 Charge and Energy#

    The continuity equation#

    Conservation of charge isn't an extra rule: it comes out of Maxwell's equations. Take the divergence of the Ampère–Maxwell law and use Gauss's law, and you get

    \[\div{\vec{J}} = -\pdv{\rho}{t}\]

    This is local conservation, which says more than “the total charge stays the same”. Total conservation alone would allow charge to vanish here and instantly reappear on the Moon. The continuity equation forbids that: charge can only leave a region by flowing out through its boundary.

    Integrating over a volume and using the divergence theorem gives

    \[\dv{Q_{\text{enc}}}{t} = -\oint \vec{J}\cdot\dd{\vec{a}}\]

    Every conservation law in this chapter has this same shape: the rate at which something increases inside a volume equals minus the rate at which it flows out through the surface. (Like a bank account: the balance changes only by money moving in or out.)

    Poynting's theorem#

    From §2.4 and §7.2, electromagnetic fields store energy with density

    \[u = \frac{1}{2}\left( \epsilon_0 E^2 + \frac{1}{\mu_0}B^2 \right)\]

    Work out the rate at which the fields do work on the charges in a volume (from the Lorentz force), and rearrange using Maxwell's equations. The result is Poynting's theorem:

    \[\dv{W}{t} = -\dv{}{t}\int_V u \dd{\tau} - \oint_S \vec{S}\cdot\dd{\vec{a}}\]

    where

    \[\vec{S} \equiv \frac{1}{\mu_0}\left( \vec{E}\cross\vec{B} \right)\]

    is the Poynting vector. It tells you which way energy is flowing and how much: energy per unit area per second (W/m²).

    In words: the work done on the charges = the energy the field loses from inside the volume − the energy that flows out through the surface. Every joule is accounted for: it either goes into the charges or leaves through the boundary.

    In differential form,

    \[\pdv{u}{t} = -\div{\vec{S}} - \vec{E}\cdot\vec{J}\]

    This has exactly the shape of the continuity equation, with \( \vec{S} \) in place of \( \vec{J} \), plus an extra term \( \vec{E}\cdot\vec{J} \) for energy handed over to the charges.

    Notice \( \vec{B} \) isn't in that hand-over term, because magnetic forces do no work. But \( \vec{B} \) is in \( \vec{S} \): carrying energy from place to place is not the same as giving it to the charges.

    Example#

    A current \( I \) flows down a cylindrical resistor of radius \( a \) and length \( L \), across which the potential difference is \( V \). Compute the Poynting vector at the surface and the total power flowing in.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    Inside a resistor carrying a current, the electric field isn't zero. It points along the axis and drives the current:

    \[E = \frac{V}{L}\,\vu{z}\]

    At the surface, Ampère's law gives the magnetic field:

    \[B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi a}\,\vu{\phi}\]

    So the Poynting vector at the surface is

    \[\vec{S} = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\left( \vec{E}\cross\vec{B} \right) = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\frac{V}{L}\frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi a}\left( \vu{z}\cross\vu{\phi} \right) = -\frac{VI}{2\pi a L}\,\vu{s}\]

    pointing inward. Multiply by the curved surface area \( 2\pi a L \):

    \[P = \left( \frac{VI}{2\pi a L} \right)\left( 2\pi a L \right) = VI\]

    which is exactly the Joule heating rate. ✓

    This is a surprising picture. The energy that heats the resistor doesn't travel inside the wire along with the current. It flows in through the sides of the resistor, from the field in the space around it. The wire guides the energy, but the field carries it.

    Example#

    Find the Poynting vector for a plane electromagnetic wave with \( \vec{E} = E_0\cos(kz-\omega t)\vu{x} \) and \( \vec{B} = (E_0/c)\cos(kz-\omega t)\vu{y} \), and its time average.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    The fields are perpendicular, so the cross product is easy:

    \[\vec{S} = \frac{1}{\mu_0}\left( \vec{E}\cross\vec{B} \right) = \frac{E_0^2}{\mu_0 c}\cos^2(kz-\omega t)\left( \vu{x}\cross\vu{y} \right) = \frac{E_0^2}{\mu_0 c}\cos^2(kz-\omega t)\,\vu{z}\]

    Energy flows along \( \vu{z} \), the way the wave is travelling, as it should.

    The average of \( \cos^2 \) over a cycle is \( 1/2 \), so

    \[\langle \vec{S} \rangle = \frac{E_0^2}{2\mu_0 c}\vu{z} = \frac{1}{2}\epsilon_0 c E_0^2 \,\vu{z}\]

    using \( c = 1/\sqrt{\mu_0\epsilon_0} \). This average power per unit area is the intensity of the wave (how bright the light is).

    In a wave the electric and magnetic energies are equal, so \( u = \epsilon_0 E^2 \). Comparing,

    \[\langle S \rangle = c \langle u \rangle\]

    This says: energy flow = energy density × speed \( c \). That's exactly what you'd expect for energy travelling along at the speed of light.

    Full derivation: §8.1.
