    9.5 Guided Waves#

    Wave guides#

    A wave guide is a hollow metal pipe that carries electromagnetic waves along it (used for microwaves in radar and satellite dishes). Treat the walls as perfect conductors. Then at the inside surface:

    \[\vec{E}^{\parallel} = \vec{0}, \qquad B^{\perp} = 0\]

    (no electric field along the wall, no magnetic field into the wall).

    Trapping the wave in a pipe changes things. Inside a guide the fields are generally not purely transverse. Solving Maxwell's equations with these boundary conditions gives all the sideways field components in terms of the components along the pipe, \( E_z \) and \( B_z \), and these can't both be zero.

    Modes are named by which one is zero:

    ModeCondition
    TE (transverse electric)\( E_z = 0 \)
    TM (transverse magnetic)\( B_z = 0 \)
    TEM\( E_z = B_z = 0 \)

    A hollow pipe cannot carry a TEM wave. Here's why, using §3.1. If \( E_z = B_z = 0 \), the sideways electric field has no curl and no divergence across the pipe, so it comes from a potential obeying Laplace's equation in 2D. The pipe wall is a single conductor, so it's all at one potential. The uniqueness theorem then says the potential is constant inside, so the field is zero. So a hollow pipe carries only TE and TM waves.

    TE waves in a rectangular guide#

    For a pipe with cross-section \( a \times b \) (with \( a > b \)), separation of variables gives modes labelled \( \text{TE}_{mn} \), with

    \[k = \sqrt{\frac{\omega^2}{c^2} - \pi^2\left( \frac{m^2}{a^2} + \frac{n^2}{b^2} \right)}\]

    where \( m \) and \( n \) are whole numbers \( 0, 1, 2, \ldots \) (not both zero). They count the half-wavelengths across the width and height.

    \( k \) is only a real number if \( \omega \) is above the cutoff frequency

    \[\omega_{mn} = c\pi\sqrt{\frac{m^2}{a^2} + \frac{n^2}{b^2}}\]

    Below cutoff, \( k \) is imaginary, and the wave dies away instead of travelling. So a wave guide is a high-pass filter: each mode only carries frequencies above its cutoff. (Roughly: the wave must be short enough to fit across the pipe.)

    The lowest cutoff is for \( \text{TE}{10} \), at \( \omega{10} = c\pi/a \). Between that and the next cutoff, only one mode can travel. Guides are normally used in this range, because different modes travel at different speeds and would smear a signal out.

    The phase and group velocities are

    \[v_p = \frac{\omega}{k} = \frac{c}{\sqrt{1 - (\omega_{mn}/\omega)^2}} > c, \qquad v_g = \dv{\omega}{k} = c\sqrt{1 - \left( \frac{\omega_{mn}}{\omega} \right)^2} < c\]

    and \( v_p v_g = c^2 \). The phase velocity is faster than light, but that's fine: nothing physical, and no information, travels at the phase velocity. The group velocity, which carries energy and signals, stays below \( c \).

    A helpful picture: the wave in a guide is an ordinary plane wave bouncing back and forth between the walls at an angle. It travels at \( c \) along its zigzag path, so its progress along the pipe is slower than \( c \). That's the group velocity.

    The coaxial cable#

    A hollow pipe can't carry TEM waves, but a guide with two separate conductors can. The uniqueness argument no longer works, because the inner wire and the outer tube can be at different potentials.

    So a coaxial cable (like a TV aerial cable) carries a TEM wave:

    \[\vec{E} = \frac{A\cos(kz-\omega t)}{s}\vu{s}, \qquad \vec{B} = \frac{A\cos(kz-\omega t)}{cs}\vu{\phi}\]

    This mode has no cutoff and travels at exactly \( c \) (in a vacuum-filled cable). \( \vec{E} \) points out from the centre wire and \( \vec{B} \) circles it, both at right angles to the cable, just like a wave in free space. That's why coax can carry signals at any frequency, right down to DC, while a hollow guide can't.

    Example#

    A rectangular wave guide has dimensions \( a = 2.28\ \text{cm} \) and \( b = 1.01\ \text{cm} \). Find the cutoff frequency of the lowest mode and the range over which only that mode propagates.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    The lowest mode is \( \text{TE}_{10} \) (\( m=1 \), \( n=0 \)):

    \[\nu_{10} = \frac{\omega_{10}}{2\pi} = \frac{c}{2a} = \frac{3.00\times10^8}{2(0.0228)} = 6.58\times10^9\ \text{Hz}\]

    About 6.6 GHz. Below this, nothing gets through.

    The next cutoffs are \( \text{TE}{20} \) at \( c/a = 13.2\ \text{GHz} \) and \( \text{TE}{01} \) at \( c/2b = 14.9\ \text{GHz} \). The lower one is \( \text{TE}_{20} \), so only one mode travels for

    \[6.6\ \text{GHz} < \nu < 13.2\ \text{GHz}\]

    That's a factor of exactly 2 in frequency (one octave), which always happens when \( a > 2b \). These are roughly the dimensions of standard “X-band” guide, used for radar.

    Single-mode operation matters because different modes have different group velocities. Sending several at once would make a short pulse arrive smeared out.

    Example#

    A signal at 10 GHz propagates in the guide above. Find the phase and group velocities, and confirm their product.

    Solution triple-click to reveal

    In the \( \text{TE}{10} \) mode, \( \nu{10} = 6.58\ \text{GHz} \), so

    \[\frac{\nu_{10}}{\nu} = \frac{6.58}{10} = 0.658, \qquad \sqrt{1 - (0.658)^2} = \sqrt{1 - 0.433} = 0.753\]

    So

    \[v_g = c(0.753) = 2.26\times10^8\ \text{m/s}\] \[v_p = \frac{c}{0.753} = 3.98\times10^8\ \text{m/s}\]

    The phase velocity is a third faster than light. That's allowed, because it's just the speed of a pattern, not of anything physical. The group velocity, three-quarters of \( c \), is how fast a pulse actually travels.

    Their product:

    \[v_p v_g = (3.98\times10^8)(2.26\times10^8) = 8.99\times10^{16} \approx c^2\]

    ✓ Note that close to cutoff, \( v_g \) becomes very small and the guide becomes strongly dispersive. So in practice guides are run comfortably above cutoff.

    Full derivation: §9.5.
